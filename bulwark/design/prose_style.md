# Bulwark — Prose Style Guide

The Ivalician register: how Bulwark's dialogue and lore are written. Read this before authoring
any dialogue, heart event, quest text, item description, or lore entry. It is the authority on
voice; `world.md` owns what is true in the world, this doc owns how it is said.

**A note on the examples.** Every do/don't example below uses invented, neutral text on purpose.
None of it is Bulwark canon, and none of it should be lifted into game content. The examples teach
a construction; they are not a well of ideas to draw from. Write your own material from the design
docs, not from this guide's demonstrations.

The empirical figures come from two localizations that built this register in English: *Final
Fantasy Tactics: The War of the Lions* (Joseph Reeder and Tom Slattery, 2007) and Alexander O.
Smith's *Vagrant Story* (2000). They are here to settle arguments, not to be hit as targets.

---

## The core

Old words, modern grammar. That is the whole trick, and it goes first because everyone who
imitates this register reaches for the grammar and ruins it.

The frontier is a commoner's world and sounds like one: short lines, rustic archaisms, plain talk
of stone and weather and debt. The crown sounds otherwise: longer, cleaner, few contractions.
Station lives in rhythm, so a capital officer and a frontier mason can disagree without either of
them raising the subject.

---

## Principles

**Old words, modern grammar.** The register lives in vocabulary and sentence shape and never
touches the verb system. *War of the Lions* runs sixty thousand words without one `thou`, `hath`,
`dost`, or `methinks`. The models are Tolkien, Gene Wolfe, and Martin, not Shakespeare. Readers
who call the register "Shakespearean" are reacting to unfamiliarity; its defenders and its critics
both quote `thou`-forms at it that appear nowhere in the source.

**Contractions do more work than every archaic word combined.**

| Expand | Keep | Add |
|---|---|---|
| the negatives and the copula: cannot · do not · will not · is not · it is · that is · let us | pronoun + auxiliary: I'll · I've · I'd · we'd · we'll · you'd · they'll | 'tis · 'twas · ne'er |

The middle column is a pattern, not a closed list: any pronoun-plus-auxiliary contraction stays.
The signature that falls out: **"I'll not." Never "I won't."**

**Archaism is garnish, never system.** Roughly one archaic word per two hundred. WotL writes
`know not` thirty-five times and `don't know` fourteen times in the same script. A rule applied
consistently reads as costume. Sample it instead.

**Dialogue is short. Chronicle is long.** Spoken lines average seven words a sentence, written lore
twenty. Same vocabulary, opposite rhythm. When a line runs long it should be because a highborn
speaker is talking, not because the register asked for it. What reads as purple is always
elaboration, never archaism. The archaic words are short Germanic monosyllables and cost the reader
nothing.

**The register drops under stress.** Ornament is a luxury of calm. A frightened man says "Run," not
"Make haste." Elaborating while the wall comes down is the most common way this voice fails. A
sentry who reports an enemy column as "two score, mayhap three" when he means "about forty" has
chosen ornament over speed; the fault is not the word but the leisure.

**Rank runs opposite to instinct.** Archaisms are lowborn.

| | Archaic words | Contractions | Line shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crown, clergy, highborn | rare | half a commoner's rate; the coldest speakers use none | long, Latinate, unbroken |
| Commoners | frequent (`aye`, `naught`, `mayhap`) | frequent | short |

Formality is not archaism. A highborn speaker is formal through *syntax*: long clauses, few
contractions. A commoner is archaic through *vocabulary*. Reversing this is what makes fantasy
casts sound alike, and it is why WotL is accused of levelling everyone when its own counts show the
opposite.

**Never break character to deliver information.** Give the speaker their own reason to say it. A
cook does not explain crop timing; he complains the larder is bare and the harvest is days off.

**If a god speaks, put it in meter.** Both Ivalice games mark divinity this way rather than with
vocabulary: in WotL, 43% of the demons' clauses land on exactly ten syllables against 7% for
humans, and the elisions that give the register away (`pow'r`, `hist'ry`) cluster there almost six
times as densely. Nobody writes "hist'ry" unless they are counting syllables. In Bulwark this is
held in reserve for the old powers of the forest and for whatever speaks through an oracle. Mortals
never scan.

**No anachronism.** No `gun`, `okay`, `process`, `system`, `resource`, `optimal`. If a word smells
of the office or the workshop floor, cut it.

## Dictionary

**Use freely** — `naught` · `aye` · `nay` · `heed` · `ere` · `aught` · `oft` · `'tis` · `'twas` ·
`mayhap` · `craven` · `sellsword` · `liege` · `fealty` · `knave` · `churl` · `wroth` · `anon` ·
`apace` · `wain` (wagon) · `nigh` · `lest` · `unto` · `fain` · `highborn` / `lowborn` / `baseborn`

**Constructions** — these carry more of the register than the word list does. Examples are generic:

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| `Yet` sentence-initial, for But / However | "Yet the gate held." |
| `past` postposed, for *ago* | "two winters past" · "many years past" |
| subjunctive inversion, for *if* | "were I you" · "had I known" |
| `for` as causal conjunction | "Bring the lantern, for the road is long." |
| `lest` | "Brace it, lest it fall." |
| negation without *do* | "I'll not" · "know not" · "matters not" · "I think not" |
| set phrases | "Well met." · "How fare you?" · "It is no matter." · "To wit," |

**Never** — `thou` · `thee` · `thy` · `thine` · `hath` · `hast` · `dost` · `wilt` · `shalt` ·
`methinks` · `prithee` · `forsooth` · `verily` · `perchance` · `betwixt` · `yonder` · `hark` ·
`wherefore` · `spake` · `hither` · `thither` · `whilst` · `withal` · `peradventure`

**Weak archaisms to avoid** — `whither` and `pray` (as *please*) score zero in the corpus; `hence`,
`make haste`, and `mark it` are unattested. `whence` survives on a single use, so keep it rare. For
*I wish that*, use `fain` or say it plainly.

**Typography.** ASCII apostrophe only. Elisions take a right-facing apostrophe: `'tis`, never the
left-facing autocorrect glyph. Editors that autocorrect a leading apostrophe produce the wrong
glyph, and `'tis` / `'twas` / `ne'er` are exactly where it happens.

## Per-character register

The one place this guide is Bulwark-specific by design: it is the character voice reference, the
thing to read before writing a given character's lines. Oaths are reflexive — characters reach for
them the way people reach for "oh god," without deciding to.

| Character | Sentences | Contractions | Archaism | Swears by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Player | short, plain | frequent | light | "aye" · "so be it" |
| Tharr | 5–8 words, declarative | frequent | rustic | the Stonefather's hand · Stone hold me · The stone remembers |
| Fenwick | long, subclauses | frequent | light, playful | Lyndra's hearth · Stars above |
| Elara | measured, exact | few | few | nothing divine — "by every road I have walked" · "mark my word" |
| Aldric | long, clean | rare | formal | the Lady's dawn · Aveline guard us · Light willing |
| Spore | short, flat | none | none | "the soil knows" · "all things return" |
| Oskar | fragmentary | frequent | rustic | names he no longer attaches to meaning |
| Arkus | short, blunt | frequent | rustic | states problems as facts; no oaths, no softening |
| Crown officers | unbroken clauses | none | rare | nothing |

Give each character a controlling metaphor and pull their whole vocabulary toward it. Tharr's is
stone (*set, course, true, plumb, foundation*); Fenwick's is the hearth (*draw, rise, keep, feed*);
Elara's is the road and the ledger (*worth, weigh, owe, run*). This is a documented technique: Smith
rebuilt a location's voice by changing one metaphor to "a spring," then reworking every word near it
(*surge, stream, well*) to match.

## The chronicle voice

For lore entries, rumors, item descriptions, and anything the player reads rather than hears.

**It attributes; it does not assert.** The highest-value habit in the register, and it is free:
`it is said` · `some say` · `is believed to` · `is thought to` · `by most accounts` · `the
trappers claim`. History is contested and the narrator was not there.

**No `I`, no `you`.** WotL's rumor entries run 2,428 words with zero first or second person.

**Hard numbers and named places.** "Two thousand years." "Five thousand strong." "Stronger than any
fort in the province." Specificity is what separates a chronicle from atmosphere.

**Documents are drier than speech, not richer.** An in-world report is flat, past-tense, and
bureaucratic — a patrol was sent, none returned, the bodies were recovered and examined — then
signed and numbered. The urge to make a document lyrical is almost always wrong.

The chronicle transformation, on a generic before/after (the technique is drawn from FFT's rumor
entries, which rewrite flat incident reports into hedged, period narration):

> Bandits are attacking travellers on the north road, and it is getting worse.

> Brigandry is on the rise along the north road, and the carters have by no means been spared. Wains
> are struck and pillaged, and those who can afford an escort now hire one.

## Forbidden constructions

The tells that mark prose as machine-written. Each is shown with a generic example — bland on
purpose, so nothing here reads as a Bulwark idea.

**The negation-flip** — denying a label, then asserting a better one. It performs insight while
adding no fact. The single most identifiable tell.

> ~~This is not a setback. It is an opportunity.~~
> ~~The keep is not abandoned. It is waiting.~~

Contrast is welcome when it carries a fact: "The map was accurate, but the roads it showed had not
been maintained in years."

**Staccato anaphora** — three or four clipped sentences marching on one frame.

> ~~The gates fell. The towers burned. The people fled.~~

**The simile parade** — more than one "X the way a Y does Z" in a passage.

> ~~She moved the way a shadow moves. He spoke the way thunder speaks.~~

**Telling the reader how to feel** — ~~"which was deeply tragic."~~ Report the fact and stop.

**Abstract predicates** — ~~"What followed was ambition dressed up as duty."~~ Name who did what.

**The paradox closer** — ~~"what began as a rescue ended as a reckoning."~~

**Tics** — `simply`, `merely`, `in the way that`, `something ancient`. Cut on sight.

**Em-dash drift** — a dash introducing a gloss after a term, as in this list, is fine. A dash
splicing two clauses because the sentence lost its shape is the tic. Commas, colons, and full stops
carry it.
