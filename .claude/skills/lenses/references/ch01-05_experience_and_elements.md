# Chapters 1 to 5: designer, experience, game, elements, theme

Working notes from Schell, The Art of Game Design, 1st edition. Pages are PDF pages. Each "To apply" line is the step to run on a design doc, screen or mechanic.

## Chapter 1. The designer (p.32 to 38)

### Start by designing
- The answer to "how do I become a designer" is to start designing now. If confidence stalls, say "I am a game designer" until you act like one, because people become what they pretend to be (p.32 to 33).
- Doubt shows up at every career stage (novice, experienced, veteran) and does not help, so the designer is told to set it aside and decide with confidence. Schell's line is that game design is decision making (p.33).
- Failure is the route to a good game. The juggler's saying applies: if you are not dropping, you are not learning (p.33).
- To apply: when a decision is stuck on "we have never done this", make the call, write it down as a test, and plan how you will find out it failed.

### The skill list (p.33 to 35)
- Schell's answer to "what skills" is "all of them". His alphabetical list: animation, anthropology, architecture, brainstorming, business, cinematography, communication, creative writing, economics, engineering, history, management, mathematics, music, psychology, public speaking, sound design, technical writing, visual arts (p.33 to 35).
- Each comes with its reason. Animation makes characters seem alive. Anthropology studies the audience in its habitat. Architecture is about people and spaces. Economics covers in-game resource economies. Mathematics covers probability, risk and scoring. Psychology is needed because the goal is to make a person happy. Sound convinces the mind it is in a place. Technical writing produces documents with no holes (p.33 to 35).
- No one masters all of them. The more you can work with, even badly, the better (p.35).
- To apply: for a target, list which of these disciplines it leans on (a shop screen leans on economics, visual arts, technical writing) and name the one nobody on the project is covering.

### Listening, the most important skill (p.35 to 37)
- Creativity is placed second. Communication has come to mean talking, and the skill underneath it is listening (p.35).
- Listening means deep, thoughtful attention to body language, tone and what goes unsaid. The Fred example: his "I'm fine" carries a request for help if you attend to how he says it (p.36).
- Thoughtful listening means asking yourself "Is that right?", "Why is it that way?", "Is this how she really feels?", "What does it mean?" (p.36).
- Brian Moriarty's point: "listen" comes from "list", tipping your head and accepting you may be knocked off balance. Deep listening accepts that what you hear may contradict what you know (p.36).
- To apply: when reading playtest notes or user feedback, write next to each comment what the person seemed to mean beyond the words, then check it against what they did.

### The five kinds of listening (p.36 to 37)
- Team (ch. 23 to 24), so the team works as one unit with the combined skills.
- Audience (ch. 8, 9, 21, 22, 30), because if players are unhappy the design failed.
- Game (most chapters), knowing it the way a mechanic knows an engine by its sound.
- Client (ch. 27 to 29), to find what they want beyond what they say.
- Self (ch. 1, 6, 32), the hardest kind and the source of creativity.
- To apply: for any review, name which of the five sources each finding came from and which source has not been consulted yet.

### The secret of the gifted (p.37 to 38)
- The minor gift is innate skill. The major gift is love of the work, which keeps you practising until skill grows past those with only the minor gift (p.37).
- The only test for the major gift is to start and see whether the work holds you (p.38).

## Chapter 2. The designer creates an experience (p.40 to 53)

### Experience is the goal (p.41 to 43)
- A designer's goal is not the game. Games are artifacts, "clumps of cardboard, or bags of bits", and are worth nothing until played. The experience during play is what the designer cares about (p.41).
- Experiences are hard to discuss because they are too familiar. They cannot be held or shared, and no two people have the same one (p.41).
- The game is not the experience. It enables it. The tree-in-the-forest example: designers care about the hearing, not the falling tree (p.41 to 42).
- Designers can only build artifacts likely to produce an experience, like building a ship in a bottle, and never see the output directly. This is why deep listening is needed (p.42).
- The split between artifact and experience is sharper in games than in books or film because players control pacing and sequence and there are random events (p.42 to 43).
- Designers accept that loss of control for feelings only games give, such as choice, freedom, responsibility, accomplishment and friendship (p.43).
- To apply: rewrite the target's goal as the experience a player should have, separate from any feature list, and flag every line of the doc that describes the artifact without saying what experience it is for.

### Three fields to borrow from (p.43 to 45)
- Psychology. Behaviorists measure behaviour from outside. Phenomenologists study experience by introspection. Science favoured the behaviorists, but designers can use both because they care about what seems true (p.43 to 44).
- Anthropology. Cultural anthropology lives among its subjects, observes, and imagines what it feels like to be them. Designers take this stance toward players by interviewing them and putting themselves in their place (p.44 to 45).
- Design. Rules of thumb from musicians, architects, filmmakers and other designers apply to games, but rarely cross between fields, so cast a wide net and be "xenophilic" (p.45).
- To apply: for one problem in the target, find one rule from another design field (film pacing, architecture, music) and test whether it applies.

### Introspection and its perils (p.45 to 48)
- Introspection is examining your own thoughts and feelings. It lets a designer judge fast what works and why (p.45 to 46).
- Peril 1. Introspection can produce false conclusions about reality. Socrates turned "learning feels like remembering" into reincarnation, and Aristotle's physics was wrong while his work on drama and ethics holds up. Designers can mostly set this peril aside because they care about how things feel (p.46 to 47).
- Peril 2. What is true for me may not be true for others: "I like playing this game, therefore it must be good" (p.47).
- The extreme "I only design for people like me" fails because designers have unusual tastes, teams disagree, and many audiences become off limits (p.47).
- The extreme "personal opinions can't be trusted" fails because early on there is nothing to playtest, testers may reject an unfinished "ugly duckling", and playtests are occasional while decisions are daily (p.47 to 48).
- The fix for peril 2 is to listen to others as well as yourself, note how their experiences differ from yours, and then predict for your audience like an anthropologist (p.48).
- To apply: for each judgment in a review, mark whether it rests on your own reaction or on observed players, and for your own reactions write how the target audience may differ.

### Dissect your feelings (p.48 to 49)
- Knowing you dislike something is not enough. State what you like, what you don't, and why. The bad-pizza friend could not say whether it was cold, soggy or over-sauced (p.48).
- After any play, analyse how it made you feel, what it made you think of, and what it made you do, and put it in words, because words are concrete and can be shared (p.48 to 49).
- To apply: replace any "feels bad" or "feels off" note in the target with the specific property at fault (too slow, unclear target, reward too small) and the moment it happens.

### Defeating Heisenberg (p.49 to 50)
- The third challenge of introspection: observing an experience disturbs it, "paralysis by analysis", like watching your fingers while typing (p.49).
- Analyze memories. Analysing a memory is easier than analysing live. Fresh or powerful memories work best, and deciding beforehand to analyse afterwards helps you recall more (p.49).
- Two passes. Play once without analysis, then go through again analysing and taking notes (p.49 to 50).
- Sneak glances. Brief checks during play, like "Exciting enough? Yes", then back in. Long inner dialogue ruins the experience (p.50).
- Observe silently. Practise watching yourself continuously without interfering, like observing your breathing in meditation (p.50).
- To apply: when testing a build, do one clean run with no notes, write down what you remember straight after, then do a second run stopping to note causes.

### Essential experience (p.51 to 52)
- A game does not need to replicate a real experience. It needs the essence: the features that define it and make it special (p.51).
- Snowball fight example. Essential parts include school cancelled, the snow packing well, cold but sunny, a big fort, Fred faking high and hitting your head, constant laughing. Non-essential parts include corduroy pants, mints in a pocket, a man walking a dog (p.51).
- The essence can arrive in forms unlike the original. Cold can come from breath puffs and shivering art, whistling wind audio, or a rule that bare hands make better snowballs until they get too cold (p.51).
- Wii Sports baseball dropped the plan to simulate all of baseball and put everything into the controller swing, leaving out nine innings and stealing bases (p.52).
- Designers who skip this rely on luck. Knowing the essence tells you which parts are safe to change and which are not (p.52).
- To apply: write the target's intended experience in one line, list its essential features, then list which element (art, sound, rule, UI) delivers each one; any essential feature with no element is a gap, and any element serving no essential feature is a candidate to cut.

### All that's real is what you feel (p.52 to 53)
- The only reality we know is our experience, which is a filtered illusion. So a designed experience can feel as real and meaningful as an everyday one (p.52 to 53).

## Chapter 3. The experience rises out of a game (p.54 to 69)

### Why define terms (p.55 to 56)
- Schell rejects the claim that missing standard definitions are a crisis. Designers explain what they mean when there is ambiguity (p.55).
- The gap is in clear thinking about ideas, not in vocabulary. What matters is saying clearly why a design is good or bad and how it can improve (p.56).
- The value of defining is the thinking it forces, not the list of terms (p.56).
- To apply: when a review uses a vague term ("fun", "deep", "clunky"), stop and write what it means for this target before going on.

### The chain of definitions (p.57 to 61)
- A game is something you play. True but too broad (p.57).
- A toy is something you play with. Then: a toy is an object you play with, since friends are not toys. Anything can be one, such as a roll of tape (p.57).
- A good toy is an object that is fun to play with (p.57).
- Fun is pleasure with surprises. Pleasure alone covers eating a sandwich or lying in the sun, which are not "fun". Test definitions with counterexamples (p.57).
- Surprise evidence: random sprays of sugar water were rated more pleasant than patterned ones with the same sugar, and unpleasant surprises still trigger pleasure centres (p.58).

### Earlier definitions of play and what Schell keeps (p.58 to 61)
- Schiller, "the aimless expenditure of exuberant energy". Rejected as the outdated surplus-energy theory, and play has goals, so "aimless" is wrong (p.58).
- Gilmore, play brings pleasure, exhilaration, power and self-initiative. Incomplete (misses imagination, competition, problem solving) and too broad, since an executive landing a contract fits (p.58 to 59).
- Salen and Zimmerman, "free movement within a more rigid structure". Covers almost everything, including a child forced to scrub a floor, but is a useful way to look at a game (p.59).
- Santayana, done spontaneously and for its own sake. Spontaneity is often but not always present, since softball is planned. Mergen's view that competitive games are not play is called too extreme. Kept: "for its own sake", meaning attitude decides whether an activity is work or play (p.59).
- Rico Medellin turned a 43-second assembly task into play by trying to beat his record, reaching a best average of 28 seconds. The change was in his reason for doing it (p.60).
- Santayana again: work and play map to servitude and freedom. The more obligated you are, the more it feels like work. "Whoever must play cannot play" (p.60).
- Schell's own: play is manipulation that indulges curiosity. Play answers questions such as "What happens when I turn this knob?" or "How many times can I jump this rope?", by willful action on something (p.60 to 61).
- To apply: for a mechanic, write the question the player is trying to answer while using it, and check that the player chooses to ask it rather than being forced to.

### Earlier definitions of game and what Schell keeps (p.62 to 65)
- Avedon and Sutton-Smith, voluntary control systems with a contest of powers, confined by rules, producing a disequilibrial outcome. Yields Q1 entered willfully, Q2 goals, Q3 conflict, Q4 rules, Q5 can be won and lost (p.62).
- Costikyan, "an interactive structure of endogenous meaning that requires players to struggle toward a goal". Yields Q6 interactive, Q7 challenge, Q8 can create their own internal value (p.62 to 64).
- Endogenous means internally generated. Monopoly money only matters inside Monopoly. Roulette makes so little endogenous value that people only play it for real money, while some MMO items sell for real money (p.63).
- Bubsy's yarn balls only raised a score, so players stopped collecting them. Sonic 2's rings protect you and give a life per hundred, so players care about them (p.64).
- Fullerton, Swain and Hoffman, a closed, formal system that engages players in structured conflict and resolves in an unequal outcome. Yields Q9 engage players and Q10 closed, formal systems. "Closed" is the boundary Huizinga called the magic circle (p.64 to 65).
- Alan Kay's warning about subroutines with more than ten arguments: a ten-item list means the ideas need regrouping (p.65).

### Games as problem solving (p.65 to 68)
- The answer players give that fits every game is "I like solving problems". Any goal poses a problem (p.65 to 66).
- Craps is a problem of taking the right calculated risks (p.66).
- War has no choices, but children treat it as "Can I control fate?" and try superstitious tricks. Once they learn fate cannot be controlled, it stops being a game and becomes an activity (p.66).
- Social contact and exercise may improve a game but are not essential. Remove problem solving and it is no longer a game (p.66).
- Problem Solving 101 maps the steps onto the qualities. State the goal (Q2), frame the problem and its rules (Q4), build a simplified interactive model (Q6) that is a closed formal system (Q10), work against challenge (Q7) and conflict (Q3), become engaged (Q9), give internal importance to things in the problem space (Q8), then win or lose (Q5) (p.67).
- The magic circle is the mind's internal problem-solving space (p.67).
- "A game is a problem-solving activity" is too broad, since it covers work and misses Q1 (p.67 to 68).
- Final definition: a game is a problem-solving activity, approached with a playful attitude (p.68).

### The summary set (p.68 to 69)
- Fun is pleasure with surprises. Play is manipulation that satisfies curiosity. A toy is an object you play with. A good toy is an object that is fun to play with. A game is a problem-solving activity, approached with a playful attitude (p.68).
- The definitions only matter if they produce insight. Disagreeing with them is a sign of thinking (p.68 to 69).
- To apply: run the ten qualities Q1 to Q10 against the target and list which are absent or weak; then name the problem the player is solving and whether it stays unsolved long enough to keep being a game.

## Chapter 4. The game consists of elements (p.70 to 77)

### Designer as doctor (p.71 to 72)
- Players only need a vague idea of what a game is made of. A designer, like a doctor, must know how the parts fit and find the true cause when something goes wrong (p.71 to 72).

### The elemental tetrad (p.72 to 74)
- Mechanics are the procedures and rules: the goal, how players can and cannot try to reach it, and what happens when they try. They are what makes a game a game, since linear media have the other three. Chosen mechanics need technology to support them, aesthetics to show them clearly, and a story that makes them make sense (p.72).
- Story is the sequence of events, linear and scripted or branching and emergent. It needs mechanics that strengthen it and let it emerge, aesthetics that reinforce it, and fitting technology (p.72 to 73).
- Aesthetics are how the game looks, sounds, smells, tastes and feels. They have the most direct relationship to the player's experience and need technology that amplifies them, mechanics that put players in that world, and story that paces them (p.73).
- Technology is any material or interaction that makes the game possible, from paper and pencil to lasers. It enables some things and prohibits others. It is the medium for the other three (p.73 to 74).
- Visibility order (Figure 4.3): aesthetics most visible, mechanics and story in the middle, technology least visible. The diamond shape shows visibility, not importance (p.73 to 74).
- Other arrangements work too, such as a square for left-brain (technology, mechanics) versus right-brain (story, aesthetics), or a tetrahedron for connectedness (p.74).
- All four are essential and equal, and each affects the others. Designers favour mechanics, artists aesthetics, engineers technology, writers story, but for the game designer all four are "your piece" (p.74).

### Space Invaders through the tetrad (p.75 to 76)
- Technology. A custom motherboard made an advancing army possible (p.75).
- Mechanics. Shields both sides can destroy, a bonus saucer, two loss conditions so no time limit, near aliens worth fewer points than far ones, and the army speeding up as it shrinks (p.75).
- Story. Originally human soldiers, changed to aliens: space battles were novel, shooting people was sensitive after Death Race, futuristic graphics suited it, and descending aliens allowed shooting upward instead of a top-down view (p.75).
- Aesthetics. Three alien designs with two-frame marching, coloured plastic strips on a black-and-white screen (which worked only because each object stayed in its own band), a heartbeat sound that speeds up, a harsh crunch when hit, and the cabinet art (p.75 to 76).
- All four served one goal, the fantasy of battling an alien army, and a weakness in one prompted changes in another (p.76).
- To apply: write one paragraph per element for the target, then for each element name how it supports or undercuts the other three, and look for a fix in one element for a weakness in another.

### Skin and skeleton (p.76 to 77)
- Skin is the player's experience. Skeleton is the elements that make it. Schell warns that many designers think so much about internals that they forget the experience (p.76).
- Focus only on skin and you know how it feels but not why or how to improve it. Focus only on skeleton and you get a structure that is "beautiful in theory" and may be horrible in practice (p.76).
- The skill is holographic design: feeling the experience while seeing which elements cause it (p.76 to 77).
- To apply: for each moment you judge in the target, write the feeling and the element causing it side by side; a feeling with no cause or a mechanic with no felt effect both need work.

## Chapter 5. The elements support a theme (p.78 to 87)

### Mere games (p.79)
- People who call games meaningless still have a game that matters to them, and the experience they value comes from the game's design (p.79).
- Games can contain any other medium, but not the reverse. The goal of theme is a stronger experience, not artistic self-expression (p.79).

### Unifying themes (p.80)
- One theme makes all elements reinforce each other and gives a test for every feature: keep it if it reinforces the theme, cut it if not (p.80).
- A theme can emerge during development, but the sooner it is settled the easier decisions become (p.80).
- Two steps: figure out the theme, then use every means possible to reinforce it (p.80).
- The theme is what the game is about, the idea all elements support. Most game themes are experience-based (p.80).
- Rich Gold's elephant book: theme "what are elephants?", with elephant text and pictures and the whole book cut in the shape of an elephant (p.80).
- To apply: write the target's theme in one line, then go through its features and mark each as reinforcing, neutral or contradicting; propose a change for every contradicting item.

### Finding the theme, Pirates of the Caribbean (p.80 to 83)
- Brief: a five-minute interactive version of the ride in a DisneyQuest CAVE, with no story or goals set (p.80).
- "Pirates" was a subject, not a theme. Candidate angles were a history documentary, a battle between pirate ships, a treasure hunt, and pirates as villains to destroy (p.80 to 81).
- Research: pirate history, other pirate games, talks with the ride's creators, riding it dozens of times before opening, interviews with staff and guests. Plenty of detail, no theme. The ride tells no coherent story and leaves it to the rider (p.81).
- The theme came on the drive home while humming "a pirate's life for me": the ride is about being a pirate. Theme: the fantasy of being a pirate (p.81).
- What changed to reinforce it (p.81 to 83):
  - A new four-screen CAVE shape suited to a ship.
  - Stereoptics so eyes could focus at infinity, like being at sea.
  - 3D glasses with the side blinders cut off so peripheral vision gave a sense of motion.
  - A custom pneumatic motion platform that felt like a ship.
  - A ship's wheel and metal cannons instead of joysticks.
  - Hyper-real visuals matching the ride.
  - The ride's music, licensed.
  - A ten-speaker system, with cannon speakers placed so the blast hits the stomach.
  - Free sailing, steered so it stays exciting (detail in ch. 16).
  - No mid-game death; players were invulnerable and the ship sank dramatically at the end if hit too often, because death and respawn broke the fantasy and the interest curve. "Theme is more important than tradition."
  - Hand-painted flat treasure made to look solid on deck.
  - Room lights filtered to look like light off water.
  - Fishing-net bags for players' belongings instead of a shelf.
  - Air conditioning vents at the bow so players feel a breeze.
  - The 3D glasses were never themed by the team; park staff on their own started calling them "the Eyes of Bluebeard". A strong theme lets everyone contribute.
- Many theming details are cheap: a line of text, a colour, a sound effect (p.83).
- To apply: list every part of the target nobody thinks of as design (loading screen, error text, menu sounds, button labels) and write one way each could reinforce the theme.

### Resonance (p.84 to 86)
- Some themes are better than others. The best resonate, touching players deeply. The pirate fantasy works because nearly everyone wants to be free of obligations (p.84).
- Experience-based themes deliver an essential experience. Truth-based themes state something people believe deeply (p.84).
- Titanic's main theme is truth-based, roughly "love is more important than life, and stronger than death". A setting where nearly everyone dies suits it, and the effects make the death feel real (p.84 to 85).
- Truth-based themes are often hidden, even from the designer. Exploring your feelings until you can state the theme makes decisions and explanations to the team easier (p.85).
- Hercules: a story retold for thousands of years likely hides a truth-based theme. Labor counts varied between tellings, but every version has a hero virtuous enough to defeat death. The game stayed mostly in the Underworld and ended breaking through to the living world for the final fight with Hades. Teamwork was a sub-theme placed under the main theme (p.85).
- Toontown Online was found piece by piece. Lists produced three sub-themes: having fun with friends, escaping reality, simplicity and transcendence. Together they described play, which lacked conflict, so its opponent work was added. Theme: "work wants to destroy play, but play must survive, because play is more important". The story became robot executives (the Cogs) trying to turn Toontown into an office park, fought by Toons with gags (p.85 to 86).
- Resonant themes lift work from craft to art. Resonance cannot be found by logic; you have to feel it, which is a form of listening to yourself (p.86).
- To apply: for the target, write an experience-based theme and a candidate truth-based theme, test each by describing the game to someone and noting which ideas get a reaction, and place sub-themes under the main one.

### Back to reality (p.87)
- Not every game has a resonant theme, but even one like Super Monkey Ball gains from a unifying theme (p.87).
- "Players will never notice" misses that theme works below conscious notice. Theme is not hidden symbolism; it focuses the work on something that matters to players (p.87).

## Lenses 1 to 10
- Lens 1, Essential Experience (p.52): what experience should the player have, what is essential to it, and how can the game capture that essence; if the intended and actual experiences differ, change the game.
- Lens 2, Surprise (p.57 to 58): what will surprise players, whether story, rules, art and technology each have surprises, and whether the rules let players surprise each other and themselves.
- Lens 3, Fun (p.58): which parts are fun and why, and which parts need more fun.
- Lens 4, Curiosity (p.61): what questions the game puts in the player's mind, what makes them care about those questions, and how to make them invent more; example, a timed maze plus a new animation per maze adds "what comes next?".
- Lens 5, Endogenous Value (p.63): what players value in the game, how to make it worth more to them, and how in-game value connects to player motivation; value reflects how much players care about succeeding.
- Lens 6, Problem Solving (p.68): what problems the game asks players to solve, whether hidden problems arise during play, and how the game can generate new problems so players return.
- Lens 7, Elemental Tetrad (p.74): whether the design uses all four element types, whether strengthening one or more would improve it, and whether the four are in harmony toward a common theme.
- Lens 8, Holographic Design (p.77): with elements and experience in view at once, which elements make the experience enjoyable, which detract, and how changing elements would improve it.
- Lens 9, Unification (p.84): what the theme is and whether every possible means is used to reinforce it; pair it with Lens 7 to inspect each element against the theme.
- Lens 10, Resonance (p.87): what feels powerful and special about the game, which ideas excite people when you describe it, what it would be with no constraints, and what is driving your instincts about how it should be.
