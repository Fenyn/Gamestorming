# Bulwark — The World

## The shape of things

This is a post-war world. Not recently post-war — the fighting ended a generation ago — but
the scars have not healed and the peace has not settled into anything comfortable. The
hegemony and the elven nations fought, and neither side won, and neither side can claim the
moral high ground. What remains is mutual exhaustion dressed up as diplomacy and an empire
that has been quietly contracting ever since.

The frontier is where the contraction shows first. The garrisons pulled back. The roads fell
apart. The chapels emptied. The people who could leave did. The ones who stayed are stubborn,
or stuck, or have nowhere else to go. The outpost the player is sent to reinforce is one of
the last surviving structures from an era when the crown still cared about what lay beyond the
capital walls.

The world runs on the tension between what people build and what time reclaims. Stone against
rot. Hearth against cold. Dawn against shadow. The cycle of death and regrowth. Every deity,
every culture, every character in the cast is positioned somewhere along that axis.

---

## The Hegemony and the Crown

The hegemony is the political order that unified the human kingdoms under a single crown. At
its height, under Arthur's reign, the hegemony was vast and prosperous. Trade routes ran deep
into the frontier. Aveline's chapels rose in every settlement. The dwarves built the
infrastructure — roads, walls, bridges, fortifications — and the humans filled them with
commerce, governance, and ambition.

Arthur's reign ended. The histories disagree on when, precisely, and on how. What is agreed
upon is that nothing that followed matched what came before.

The wars accelerated the decline. The hegemony poured its strength into the conflict with the
elven nations, and the dwarves — as always — served as infrastructure. Engineers, builders,
fortifiers. They built the siege works and the supply lines and the walls, and when the walls
came down they were standing behind them.

The war ended in a cold peace. Not a treaty of equals but a mutual admission that neither
side had the strength or the stomach to continue. The hegemony withdrew from its furthest
holdings. The garrisons pulled back. The crown turned inward, managing decline rather than
building anything new. The frontier, which had once been the edge of something growing, became
the edge of something shrinking.

The crown still exists. It is not evil. It is simply diminished — a government that makes
sensible decisions in marble halls and does not look too closely at what those decisions cost
the people who live beyond them.

## The Wars

The conflict between the hegemony and the elven nations was fought over territory, resources,
and the usual accumulation of grievances that builds when two civilizations share a continent
and disagree about whose claim to it runs deeper.

The wars were long enough to become normal and ugly enough to leave marks that will not fade
in a single lifetime. Both sides did things they will not speak of easily. The hegemony's
soldiers followed orders that should not have been given. The elves, in a single battle that
ended the war more than any treaty, deployed a divine weapon of mass destruction — channeled
something sacred, something meant for miracles, and turned it into an instrument of killing.
The blast annihilated everything within its radius and damaged things that should not have been
damageable, including the minds of the survivors who absorbed divine energy they were never
meant to hold.

The peace is mutual shame. The elves desecrated something holy to win a battle. The hegemony
committed acts that earned the desecration. Neither side can look the other in the eye without
seeing a mirror. Trade has resumed, barely. Diplomacy functions, barely. Trust does not exist.

Elara is an elf who trades in hegemony territory. She walks through settlements that lost
sons to a war her people ended with an atrocity, and she does so with the practiced ease of
someone who has been navigating that tension for longer than most humans have been alive. She
does not apologize. She does not explain. She simply continues, because continuing is the only
alternative to stopping, and she has never been inclined to stop.

## The Frontier

The frontier is the empire's edge — the borderlands where civilization thins and the wild
begins. Once it was garrisoned, patrolled, maintained. Chapels of Aveline stood in every
hamlet. Dwarven-built roads connected the outposts. Farmers worked the soil. Traders ran the
routes. It was not the heartland, but it was held.

Now it is not. The garrisons withdrew. The farmers left. The roads cracked. The chapels stand
empty or half-ruined, tended only by the faithful few who refused to abandon them. What remains
is a landscape reclaiming itself — overgrown, hollowed out, returning to what it was before
the crown decided to build upon it.

### The Elderwood

The great forest that borders the outpost. It has many names along the frontier — Thornwood
near the outpost, Elderwood in the deeper reaches — but it is one continuous thing, and it is
old. Older than the hegemony. Older than the wars. The trees remember a time before the roads,
and they have been waiting patiently for the roads to leave.

The forest is not hostile in the way a monster is hostile. It is indifferent in the way that
something ancient is indifferent. It does not hate the outpost. It simply does not recognize
the outpost's right to exist, and in the absence of the garrison that held the boundary, the
forest has been pressing inward season by season, reclaiming what was cleared.

Within the deep Elderwood, older things stir. Grandmother Mulch has been tending the cycle of
rot and regrowth since before any tree currently standing was a seed. Fey connections flicker
at the edges — doors that open and close at their own pleasure, leaving gnomes stranded on the
wrong side. Leshies grow from the forest floor, animated by spirits older than memory.

The outpost was not built in this location by accident. It was placed here because this is
where civilization's line was drawn. Somewhere nearby, the forest's edge was once a border,
negotiated or imposed, between the settled world and something that predates settlement
entirely. The garrison that held that line is gone. What the outpost is doing, whether the
player knows it yet or not, is rebuilding the bulwark.

### What presses at the edges

The forest grows bolder because the line is no longer held. This is not a single threat with
a name. It is the accumulated pressure of a wild place that has been held at arm's length for
generations and is now, slowly, testing whether the arm is still there. Creatures venture
closer. The undergrowth advances. The old paths grow over. The silence of an empty border
invites things that once kept their distance.

This is not an invasion. It is an ecosystem rebalancing. The outpost's job, and the player's
job, is to re-establish the balance — not to conquer the forest, but to hold the line firmly
enough that both sides of it can exist.

## The Age of Shadow and the Dawn

Before the hegemony, before the crown, before the roads and the walls and the chapels, the
world was dark. Not metaphorically. The long shadow hung over everything, and the dead walked
freely, and the living huddled where they could.

Aveline set her hand against the horizon and the sun came. With it came warmth, and the slow
green mending of all wounded things. This is the foundational myth of the hegemony's faith —
the Lady of Dawn is not merely a goddess of healing. She is the reason there is a world worth
healing. Her chapels, now half-ruined along the frontier, are monuments to the idea that the
darkness was pushed back once and can be pushed back again.

Whether the Age of Shadow is literal history or theological metaphor depends on who you ask.
The faithful — Tharr, Aldric, Sister Cael — treat it as fact. The scholars debate. The
practical — the player, Elara — simply note that the chapels exist and the darkness they were
built against has not entirely gone away.

## The Divine

The gods are present. Not abstractly present — present the way the weather is present. Their
power flows through clerics and champions and oracles who did not ask for it. Their chapels are
built with real stone by real hands. Their edicts and anathema carry real consequences.

### Aveline, the Lady of Dawn
The first light. Compassion expressed through action. Her faithful carry lanterns into dark
places and offer the open hand before the closed fist. Her chapels ran through the hegemony
like a golden thread during Arthur's reign. Now they stand half-ruined along the frontier,
tended by those who will not leave. Aldric knelt in one and rose as a Champion. Tharr's outpost
once had one. It can again.

### Valdûr, the Stonefather
The earth itself. His body is the world — mountains his spine, veins of ore his blood. Dwarves
believe they were carved from his flesh and owe a debt of craft in return. Prayer is done with
a hammer in hand. Temples are workshops. A perfectly laid wall is a hymn. Tharr's faith is
Valdûr's faith: the quality of your work reflects the quality of your soul. "If the stones
hold, the Stonefather approves."

### Lyndra, the Hearthkeeper
The halfling goddess who showed up, sat down, and never left. Kinship, the hearth, the shared
meal. Her faith has no formal clergy — any halfling who cooks for others is performing a rite.
Fenwick's kitchen IS his temple, and every meal is an offering. "A full belly and a warm fire.
What else does anyone need?"

### Grandmother Mulch
Not a deity. A patron — the oldest thing in the forest, the process by which the forest
digests itself. Rot that feeds the root. Decay that becomes growth. She is kind on a timescale
that does not always feel kind to mortals. She does not oppose civilization. She opposes waste.
Spore is her vessel, her witch, the small necessary work that the old thing is too large and
too ancient to do herself.

## The Prose Voice

The elevated register of the world's speech is not a costume. It is the natural voice of a
civilization that remembers its own history. These people grew up hearing songs about Arthur's
golden age. They invoke Aveline's dawn in their oaths. They swear by Valdûr's stone. The
formality is cultural inheritance — the weight of a world that was once great and knows,
somewhere beneath the daily work of surviving, that it is less than it was.

### Principles

**The formality is earned, not imposed.** Characters speak this way because their world speaks
this way. A farmer's son who joined the army still says "aye" instead of "yes" and "naught"
instead of "nothing" — not because he is performing, but because that is how people talk where
he comes from. The register is the water they swim in.

**Every character inhabits the register differently.** Fenwick uses formality the way an
academic uses jargon — comfortably, with occasional flourishes that amuse him. Elara uses it
the way a merchant uses a scale — precisely, with no wasted weight. Tharr uses it the way a
mason uses mortar — sparingly, to hold something in place. The player uses it the way a
soldier uses a blade — direct, efficient, nothing decorative.

**Gravity lives in simplicity.** The most powerful moments use the shortest words. "The stone
held. I stood beside it." The register rises for banter and description. It drops to its
simplest for truth.

**Wit operates within the register, not outside it.** Characters do not break voice to be
funny. Fenwick's humor is funnier because it comes in full sentences. Elara's dry observations
land harder because they are delivered with the same precision she uses for everything else.

### Vocabulary

Prefer these over their modern equivalents when the sentence allows it naturally:

| Use | Instead of |
|---|---|
| aye | yes |
| nay | no |
| naught | nothing |
| whither | where (to) |
| whence | where (from) |
| hence | from here / therefore |
| ere | before |
| 'tis | it is |
| pray | please (as interjection) |
| mayhap | maybe / perhaps |
| I shall | I will |
| would that | I wish that |
| mark (it) | note / notice |
| make haste | hurry |

Do NOT use: thee/thou/thy (too archaic, breaks flow), forsooth, verily, prithee (too
performative), hath/doth (too Shakespearean).

### Oaths and invocations

Characters swear by what they believe in. These are reflexive — people say them the way we
say "oh my god" or "for god's sake," without thinking:

- **Tharr**: "By the Stonefather's hand" / "Stone hold me" / "The stone remembers"
- **Fenwick**: "By Lyndra's hearth" / "Stars above" / invokes warmth and light
- **Aldric**: "By the Lady's dawn" / "Aveline guard us" / "Light willing"
- **Elara**: No divine oaths (secular). Uses precise language instead of invocations.
  Swears by observable things: "by every road I have walked" / "mark my word"
- **Player**: Minimal — grounded. If any, they are simple: "aye" / "so be it"
- **Spore**: Does not swear. States things as Grandmother Mulch might: "the soil knows" /
  "all things return"
- **Oskar**: Once swore by Valdûr (he is a dwarf). Now swears by nothing, or by habit
  invokes names he has begun to forget the meaning of — which is heartbreaking.

### Sentence rhythm

Favor the patterns of formal English prose — not Shakespearean, but pre-modern. Sentences
that have weight and cadence:

Instead of: "The road is falling apart."
Write: "The road has not seen a mason's hand in years."

Instead of: "Nobody lives here anymore."
Write: "Those who remained are of a particular sort. Stubborn. Or without the means to leave."

Instead of: "It looks terrible."
Write: "Portions of it stand. Other portions have entered into negotiation with the earth
regarding their continued verticality."

The rhythm varies by character. Fenwick's sentences are longer, with subclauses and qualifiers.
Elara's are measured and precise. Tharr's are short, declarative, stone-heavy. The player's
fall between — longer than Tharr, shorter than Fenwick, always grounded in what they can see
and touch.

## Key tensions (thematic, not mechanical)

- **Building vs. decay**: the outpost rises while the forest presses. Neither is wrong.
- **Memory vs. present**: the hegemony remembers its golden age. The frontier lives in its
  aftermath. The gap between what was and what is drives every character differently.
- **Shame vs. purpose**: the wars left scars on every faction. The elves, the hegemony, the
  dwarves who built the machinery of it. The outpost is not just a restoration project — it is
  a chance to build something that is not defined by what came before.
- **The line**: civilization and the wild need a boundary. Not a war, not a conquest — a
  border. The outpost is that border. The bulwark.

## Open questions (for the user to decide)

- **The kingdom's name**: the political entity is currently called "the crown" / "the
  hegemony." Does it need a proper name? If so, what?
- **Arthur**: Is "Arthur" the actual name of the golden-age king, or a placeholder? What
  happened to him — death, disappearance, betrayal?
- **The war's name**: did the human-elf conflict have a name? ("The Sundering," "The
  Ashfall," "The War of the Divided Light"?)
- **Thornwood vs. Elderwood**: are these different names for the same forest (near vs. deep),
  or different forests? The character bios use both.
- **Elara's specific history**: she is an elf in post-war hegemony territory. Does she have
  a personal connection to the war, or is she simply old enough to have lived through it?
- **The Fey**: Thistle came through a Fey door. How present is the Fey in this world — rare
  and mysterious, or a known if uncommon phenomenon?
