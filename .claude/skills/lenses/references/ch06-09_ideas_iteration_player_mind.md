# Chapters 6 to 9: ideas, iteration, the player, the player's mind

Schell, The Art of Game Design, 1st edition. Page cites are PDF pages. Paraphrased notes; read the
book with `book.py pages A B` before quoting it.

## Chapter 6. The Game Begins with an Idea (p.89-105)

- The informal design method is three steps: think of an idea, try it, keep changing and testing it until it seems good enough. The book says professionals do exactly this and the craft is in doing each step well (p.89).
  - To apply, name which of the three steps the target is in before choosing any other method.
- Inspiration. An old juggler's moves could not be copied because he took them from ballet, geese and a paper punch rather than from other jugglers. Look for inspiration outside your medium (p.89-90).
  - To apply, list two or three non-game experiences the target could draw on, and drop references that are only other games in the same genre.
- Lens #11, Infinite Inspiration. Look away from games: what life experience do I want to share, and in what small way can its essence go into the game? Pairs with Lens #1; each tetrad element can draw on one inspiration or several blended (p.90-91).
- The problem statement. Design solves problems, so state the problem first, giving both goal and constraints. The example "a Web-based game teenagers will really like" is widened to "a Web-based experience" once the designer sees "game" was an assumption. Too broad misses the goal; too narrow shuts out solutions because it starts from a solution (p.91-92).
  - To apply, write one sentence of the form "How can I make [constraint] that [goal]?" for the target, then strike each word that names a solution rather than a need.
- Three advantages of a stated problem: a broader creative space, a clear measure for judging ideas (how well does it solve the problem), and easier team communication because people stop solving different problems without knowing it (p.92).
  - To apply, score each candidate idea against the problem sentence and flag any team discussion where two people are solving different problems.
- Restate the problem when exploration reveals what it was. Check the statement against the tetrad (technology, mechanics, story, aesthetics) to see where it already fixes choices; the four samples are magnets in a board game, Hansel and Gretel as a videogame, a game that feels like a surrealist painting, and improving Tetris. With no constraints at all, pick some, because picking creates a problem statement (p.92).
  - To apply, mark which tetrad elements the target's problem sentence already decides and which are still free.
- Lens #12, the Problem Statement. Treat the game as the solution to a problem: what problem am I trying to solve, what assumptions have nothing to do with its purpose, is a game the best solution and why, and how will I know it is solved (p.93).
- Sleep and Dali's "slumber with a key", a nap ended by a dropped key hitting a plate, offered as preparation for idea generation (p.93).
- The silent partner. Good ideas come from the subconscious rather than from reasoning; Kekule saw benzene's ring as a snake biting its tail in a dream. Treating the subconscious as a separate person helps, as Stephen King does with his "basement guy" muse (p.94-95).
- Five common traits of the creative subconscious: it does not talk and uses images and feelings, it is impulsive, emotional, playful, and irrational. Harpo Marx is offered as its patron saint. Its odd ideas are sometimes the missing angle (p.95-96).
  - To apply, when generating ideas for the target, keep the irrational or joke ideas on the list for one round of consideration before cutting any.
- Subconscious tip #1, pay attention. Listen to thoughts, feelings and dreams and take odd suggestions seriously for a moment; "what if the surfboards were bananas" turns into surfing monkeys (p.96-97).
- Subconscious tip #2, record your ideas all the time. Memory is poor, and written ideas stop cluttering the mind; a voice recorder is suggested, with regular transcription (p.97).
- Subconscious tip #3, manage its appetites judiciously. Unmet needs (food, safety, relationships, per Maslow) become obsessions that block creative work; feed safe appetites, curb dangerous ones (p.97-98).
- Subconscious tip #4, sleep. Sleep serves the mind, and the subconscious seems absent when the designer is short of it (p.98).
- Subconscious tip #5, don't push too hard. Like a forgotten name that surfaces later, answers come in the background; give a clear, important problem and leave it (p.98).
  - To apply, set the problem sentence down and return to the target after other work rather than forcing a same-session answer.
- A personal relationship. Each designer has to find by experiment which techniques work for them (p.99).
- The book gives fifteen brainstorming tips (the heading says "Fifteen Nitty-Gritty") (p.99-105):
  1. The write answer. Write solutions down, because memory is poor and unwritten ideas crowd out new ones (p.99).
  2. Write or type. Use whichever works; unlined paper allows circling, sketching and arrows (p.99).
  3. Sketch. Drawing, even badly, helps memory and triggers more ideas (p.99-100).
  4. Toys. Bring related and unrelated toys, or clay, to engage sight and touch (p.100).
  5. Change your perspective. Brainstorm in other places and postures (p.100).
  6. Immerse yourself. Study the audience, the technology, other versions of the story, and games using the mechanic (p.100).
  7. Crack jokes. Humour opens new angles; whoever derails the group puts it back on track, "He who derails, rerails" (p.100-101).
  8. Spare no expense. Do not let cheap materials limit you; big writing on one side of good paper lets you lay sheets out (p.101).
  9. The writing on the wall. Use whiteboards, index cards or large sheets everyone can see, that can be stored and reposted later (p.101).
  10. The space remembers. Spatial memory beats list memory, so ideas posted around a room come back when you return (p.101-102).
  11. Write everything. Write every idea, including stupid ones, and do not censor yourself or others (Pauling: many ideas lead to good ones) (p.102).
  12. Number your lists. Numbers make items easier to discuss and lend them weight (p.102-103).
  13. Mix and match categories. Make separate lists per tetrad element (example: a game for teenage girls) and combine entries across lists; invent other categories as needed (p.103-104).
  14. Talk to yourself. Saying ideas aloud makes them more concrete (p.104).
  15. Find a partner. A good partner speeds things up; groups of four or fewer, narrow problems, and avoid people who poke holes in everything or have narrow tastes (p.104-105).
  - To apply, produce a numbered list of ideas for the target, split by tetrad element, then generate at least three cross-list combinations before judging any.
- After brainstorming, a designer should have dozens of ideas on any topic; the next step is narrowing them (p.105).

## Chapter 7. The Game Improves Through Iteration (p.106-126)

- Choosing an idea. Indecision stalls designers. Commit quickly, because commitment exposes flaws and benefits, then reverse without sentiment when it proves wrong; ideas are "paper cups", not fine china (p.107).
  - To apply, pick one direction for the target now, list the consequences that become visible, and state what finding would make you drop it.
- The eight filters. A design is good enough only when it passes all eight; any change sends it back through all of them, since fixing one can break another (p.107-109):
  1. Artistic impulse: does this game feel right? (p.108)
  2. Demographics: will the intended audience like it enough? (p.108)
  3. Experience design: is this a well-designed game, by the standards of aesthetics, interest curves, theme, balance and the other lenses? (p.108)
  4. Innovation: is it novel enough? (p.108)
  5. Business and marketing: will it sell, considering box readability, genre expectations, competitors, cost and retail? (p.108)
  6. Engineering: is it technically possible to build, noting that engineering can also suggest features? (p.108-109)
  7. Social/community: does it meet our social and community goals? (p.109)
  8. Playtesting: do playtesters enjoy it enough? Called arguably the most important, and it retunes the other filters (p.109)
  - To apply, answer each of the eight questions for the target with a yes, no or unknown and the evidence, and mark every unknown as a risk.
- Changing a filter, such as retargeting the demographic, is allowed when constraints permit; the goal is to get through all eight somehow. When choosing among ideas, prefer the one most likely to survive the filters (p.109).
- Lens #13, the Eight Filters. The design is finished only when it passes all eight questions without needing a change; add filters for special cases, such as "does it teach what it should" for an educational game (p.109-110).
- The rule of the loop: the more times you test and improve a design, the better the game. The book calls it an absolute truth with no exceptions, not a lens. Long, costly loops are the core risk of videogame development (p.110-111).
- The two loop questions: how can I make every loop count, and how can I loop as fast as possible? (p.111)
  - To apply, for the target's next change, state what that loop will teach and how many days it takes, then look for a way to halve the time.
- Warning against the waterfall model: a linear seven-step process that skips iteration. Its one merit is more planning up front; Royce's original paper itself called for going back to earlier steps (p.111-113).
- Boehm's spiral model (1986) has three ideas inside it: risk assessment, prototypes and looping. Steps: make a basic design, find its greatest risks, build prototypes that mitigate them, test the prototypes, make a more detailed design from what you learned, return to step 2. It answers the loop questions: assess and mitigate risks so each loop counts, build many rough prototypes so loops are fast (p.113-114).
  - To apply, run the six spiral steps on the target and write the result of step 2 as a ranked list before building anything.
- Example, Prisoners of Bubbleville: a tetrad design brief for a parachuting-cat game, then a risk list of five items: the core mechanic may not be fun, the engine may not draw the city, thirty house interiors may not fit the schedule, players may not like the characters and story, and the publisher may force a movie retheme (p.114-115).
- Risk mitigation means reducing or removing each risk as early as possible, often with small prototypes. In the example: an abstract 2D shapes version of the mechanic in a week or two; a throwaway engine test with the right object count and no gameplay; one finished house and character to time the art cost; concept art and storyboards shown to target players; and for the retheme, press management for a decision or design so a retheme is easy (p.115-116).
  - To apply, write each risk of the target as a sentence, then give each the cheapest test or decision that would settle it, including non-prototype actions.
- Lens #14, Risk Mitigation. Stop thinking positively: what could keep this game from being great, and how do we stop it? Work on the parts in danger rather than the parts you feel sure of (p.117).
- Eight tips for productive prototyping (p.117-121):
  1. Answer a question. Every prototype answers one or more stated questions, such as how many characters the tech supports or whether the core play stays fun; do not overbuild (p.117-118).
  2. Forget quality. Rough is fine and polish can hide problems, because testers criticise rough things more freely (p.118).
  3. Don't get attached. Brooks: "Plan to throw one away"; keep the pieces that work (p.118).
  4. Prioritize your prototypes. Biggest risks first, and "upstream" prototypes whose result could void the others go first (p.118).
  5. Parallelize prototypes productively. Tech, art and gameplay prototypes can run at the same time (p.119).
  6. It doesn't have to be digital. Paper prototypes; Toontown's combat was balanced as a board game; Tetris from cardboard in about 15 minutes; Doom on graph paper with people as monsters and a five-second metronome tick (p.119-120).
  7. Pick a "fast loop" game engine. Prefer changing code while the game runs (clay) over the compile-run-navigate cycle (bread); put fast static code low and dynamic scripting high (p.120-121).
  8. Build the toy first. Make sure the toy is fun before building a game on it; Lemmings started as a world of walking creatures, and Grand Theft Auto as a city "medium" that borrowed Pac-Man's maze rules (p.121).
  - To apply, for each risk on the target's list, write the one question its prototype answers, rank the prototypes by risk and dependency, and state which can be paper.
- Lens #15, the Toy. Ask whether the game is fun to play with, not to play: would it be fun with no goal, and do people want to touch it before they know what to do? Use it to add toy qualities to an existing game or to invent toys first (p.121-122).
- The formal loop: state the problem, brainstorm solutions, choose one, list its risks, build prototypes to mitigate them, test them and stop if good enough, otherwise state the new problems and return to brainstorming (p.122).
  - To apply, write the target's current position in these seven steps and the new problem statement the last test produced.
- Example, the racing game loops: "new racing game" leads to submarine races, prototypes of tracks, mechanics and water effects, and results such as 60% underwater, 20% air, 20% near the surface, and avoiding rapid-fire guns for networking; the problem becomes "racing subs", then "flying dinos". Problem statements grow more specific each loop, parallel work made six loops out of two and a half, and the whole team shaped the design (p.122-125).
- How much is enough. One more loop always helps, so the work is abandoned rather than finished; aim for enough loops to be proud of the game within budget. Nobody can estimate the finish at the first loop because the game is not yet known (p.125).
- Mark Cerny's "The Method": you are in pre-production until two publishable levels with all features are complete, which usually costs about 30% of the budget; $1 million to reach that point implies about $2.3 million more. Only then can the rest be scheduled (p.125-126).
  - To apply, ask whether the target has one finished, shippable slice with every feature; if not, treat schedule estimates as guesses.

## Chapter 8. The Game Is Made for a Player (p.128-143)

- Einstein's violin. Asked to lecture on relativity to a lay audience, he played the violin instead, because what they wanted was an encounter with Einstein. Players often do not know what they will enjoy, so the designer must know it better than they do (p.129).
- Project yourself. Walt Disney crouched while touring Disneyland to see it at a child's eye height. Adopt the player's mental perspective too. If you were once in the audience, revive your memories of that age; if not, study people you know in it and spend time with them like an anthropologist (p.130).
  - To apply, describe the target screen or mechanic from the eye level and knowledge of a named player type, listing what they see first and what they would not know.
- Demographics are groupings by shared traits; for games, age and gender matter most (p.130-131).
- The nine age brackets the industry uses (p.131-133):
  - 0-3, infant/toddler: interested in toys; games are too complex (p.131).
  - 4-6, preschooler: first interest in simple games, played mostly with parents who bend the rules (p.131).
  - 7-9, kids: the "age of reason"; can read and solve hard problems, and start choosing their own games (p.132).
  - 10-13, preteen or tween: the "age of obsession"; deeper thought and passionate interests, often games for boys (p.132).
  - 13-18, teen: male and female interests split, boys toward competition and mastery, girls toward real-world issues and communication; both experiment with new experiences (p.132).
  - 18-24, young adult: play less than children, have settled tastes, and have time and money (p.132).
  - 25-35: time gets scarce at "peak family formation"; mostly casual players, but hardcore gamers here buy a lot and influence others (p.132).
  - 35-50: "family maturation"; casual players who make the expensive purchases and look for family play (p.132).
  - 50 and up, empty nesters: more free time; return to old games or try new ones, with strong interest in social play such as golf, bridge and online games (p.132-133).
  - To apply, name the target's bracket and check each feature against what the book says that bracket wants.
- Younger brackets are separated by mental development, older ones by family transitions. All play links back to childhood, so speak "the language of their childhood" (p.133).
- Gender. The book says the sexes differ in interests and skills, that the origin does not matter to a designer, and that changing violent aesthetics while keeping the mechanics has mostly failed. It cites Koster's view that mastering abstract formal systems appeals more to males, while that core can carry story, creativity, learning and socialising that appeal to both, "like apples" (p.133-134).
- Five things the book says males like to see in games: mastery for its own sake (females prefer mastery with a purpose), competition (for females the bad feeling of losing or making others lose can outweigh winning), destruction, spatial puzzles, and trial and error over instructions (p.134-135).
- Five things the book says females like to see in games: emotion as an end in itself, connection to the real world (Barbie Fashion Designer outsold the fantasy Barbie as Rapunzel; the Sims), nurturing (Toontown let players heal only others), dialog and verbal puzzles, and learning by example through clear step-by-step tutorials. It adds that females handle parallel tasks more easily (p.135-136).
- The book presents these as generalisations useful for large audiences, not rules for individuals (p.134).
  - To apply, list which of the ten items the target offers and which its intended audience would miss.
- Example, Hasbro's Pox: girls rarely play informal competitive games in large groups because disputes split them and stop play, so the social but competitive design was aimed at boys only (p.136).
- Early computer games removed social, verbal and emotional content, were divorced from the real world, hard to learn, and offered unlimited destruction, which the book links to their mostly male audience (p.137).
- Lens #16, the Player. Think about the player, not the game: what do they like and dislike and why, what do they expect, what would I want in their place, and what would they like or dislike about this game? Hold it together with the Lens of Holographic Design, and watch people play (p.137).
- Example, Pirates of the Caribbean at DisneyQuest. Boys played offensively. Girls defended the ship, liked treasure (so it was piled on deck and skeletons stole it in the final battle) and talked in huddles. Men were "tall boys with credit cards" who planned optimal play. Mothers took the wheel at the back, so steering was made easy for non-gamers and gave them a way to manage the family's fun. Solutions came from prototyping and playtesting (p.138-139).
  - To apply, split the target's audience into its subgroups and write how each would play the same feature, then name one change that serves the weakest-served group.
- Psychographics group players by what they find pleasurable inside rather than by external traits. Some are lifestyle groups (dog lover, hardcore FPS player); others are pleasure preferences, since all motivation traces to pleasure seeking (p.139).
- LeBlanc's eight game pleasures (p.140-141):
  1. Sensation: using the senses, delivered mainly by aesthetics; Costikyan's Axis & Allies became fun with plastic pieces. It improves a good game but cannot save a bad one (p.140).
  2. Fantasy: the imaginary world and being someone else (p.140).
  3. Narrative: a dramatic unfolding of events, not necessarily a fixed story (p.140).
  4. Challenge: a core game pleasure, enough for some players (p.140).
  5. Fellowship: friendship, cooperation, community (p.140).
  6. Discovery: finding anything new, from places to secret strategies (p.140).
  7. Expression: self-expression and creating things, often unrelated to the game's goals (p.140-141).
  8. Submission: entering the magic circle; the best worlds suspend disbelief for you (p.141).
- Bartle's four player types with card-suit mnemonics: Achievers (diamonds) want the game's goals and Challenge; Explorers (spades) want its breadth and Discovery; Socializers (hearts) want relationships and Fellowship; Killers (clubs) want to compete with and defeat others, a mix of competition and destruction that Bartle describes as "imposing themselves on others" and that also includes helpers. On Bartle's graph, Achievers act on the world, Explorers interact with the world, Socializers interact with players, Killers act on players (p.141-142).
  - To apply, map each activity in the target to a Bartle quadrant and a LeBlanc pleasure, and report empty quadrants and missing pleasures.
- Warning: these taxonomies have gaps and can hide pleasures such as destruction and nurturing. Further pleasures listed: anticipation, delight in another's misfortune (schadenfreude), gift giving, humour, possibility, pride in an accomplishment (naches), purification (clearing a level), surprise, thrill ("fear minus death equals fun"), triumph over adversity (fiero), and wonder. Keep an eye out for pleasures no list names (p.142-143).
- Lens #17, Pleasure. What pleasures does the game give and can they improve, and what pleasures are missing, why, and can they be added? Look for unclassified pleasures that could make the game distinct (p.143).

## Chapter 9. The Experience Is in the Player's Mind (p.144-159)

- Most of the mind's work is hidden. The "boast, boast, boast; what goes in a toaster?" exercise shows priming makes people answer "toast" (p.145).
- Four mental abilities make gameplay possible: modeling, focus, imagination and empathy. The chapter then covers the subconscious mind's priorities (motivation) (p.146).
- Modeling. Minds work with simplified models of reality, not reality; colours, the visible spectrum and ignored mites are examples (p.146-147).
- Charlie Brown reads as a person because he fits our internal models: a big head because we store more about faces, and outlines because the visual system already draws object boundaries, so cartoons are "pre-digested" and restful. Magicians break our models, which causes the gasp (p.147-148).
- Games are pre-digested models with complexity stripped out, which makes them relaxing; abstract games are bare models, RPGs coat the model in pleasing aesthetics, and games give practice with simpler models before real ones (p.148).
- Whoever controls how the illusion forms in the player's mind can make experiences that feel as real as reality (p.148-149).
  - To apply, write the simple model a player would form of the target mechanic in three or four rules, then compare it with how the system actually works and list the mismatches.
- Focus. The brain attends selectively (the cocktail party effect, dichotic listening studies). Focus is set by unconscious desire and conscious will; the designer's aim is to hold it as long and as intensely as possible (p.149).
- Flow (Csikszentmihalyi) is sustained focus with enjoyment, no intrusive thoughts and lost track of time. Its conditions: clear goals, no distractions, direct feedback, and continuous challenge the player believes is achievable (p.149-150).
  - To apply, check the target against the four conditions and name the moment where each is weakest.
- The flow channel sits between boredom (skill above challenge) and anxiety (challenge above skill). In the tennis example Alex starts in flow, grows bored as skill rises or anxious against a stronger opponent, and returns by raising the challenge or his skill; flow at a higher level is more complex but also unstable, which drives growth (p.150-151).
- Rising difficulty levels are self-balancing because skilled players pass early levels quickly; most players still quit in the frustration zone, and whether that is bad is debated (p.151).
- A path that rises straight up the channel is acceptable, but a cycle of rising challenge, a reward of more power, an easier stretch, then rising challenge again feels better. The example is a gun upgrade from three shots to two, followed by enemies needing three or four. This "tense and release" gives variety and anticipation (p.152-153).
  - To apply, sketch the target's challenge level over a session or run and mark where reward releases tension; flag long flat or steadily rising stretches.
- Lens #18, Flow. What holds the player's focus: are the goals clear, are they the goals I intended, do distractions make players forget goals and can those be cut or tied to the goals, is there a steady stream of challenge matched to rising skill, and are skills improving at the rate hoped? (p.153)
- Testing for flow takes long observation, not ten minutes, and a game can stop producing flow on later plays. Flow often looks like quiet withdrawal in solo play or constant game talk in multiplayer; watch for the event that knocks the player out. Also apply flow to your own design time (p.153-154).
  - To apply, in a playtest note the timestamp each player goes quiet and focused and the event that ends it, and treat those events as the fix list.
- Empathy. People project into others and take on their feelings; actors meeting eyes with emotion-projecting actors copy the expression unbidden. Dogs evolved expressive faces that capture empathy. Because we empathise with our models of others, drawings and game characters work as well as people, and film moves empathy between characters (p.154-155).
- In games, players project their decision making into a character as well as their feelings, which non-interactive media cannot do; empathic projection is also a problem-solving tool (p.155).
  - To apply, track where the player's empathy points at each beat of the target scene and note any beat where it has no clear target.
- Imagination, meaning everyday gap-filling. "The mailman stole my car yesterday" makes the listener supply the mailman's look, the car's colour, the time and the motive, and revise them when told it was a toy car. Games do not need every detail; the skill is choosing what to show and what to leave to the player (p.155-156).
- Imagination has two functions, communication (often storytelling) and problem solving, so the designer treats the player's imagination as a storytelling partner and judges what problems it can solve (p.157).
  - To apply, list what the target shows explicitly and what it leaves to the player, and move one shown detail to implied or one confusing implied detail to shown.
- Motivation through Maslow's hierarchy: physiological, safety, belonging-love, self-esteem, self-actualisation. Higher needs are pursued only once lower ones are met (p.157).
- Maslow as applied: most game activity (achievement, mastery) sits at level four, self-esteem. Multiplayer games meet the more basic level three, which explains their pull. Games with communities and creation tools hit levels three, four and five, and levels can feed each other (p.157-158).
  - To apply, place each of the target's activities on a Maslow level and say whether the game delivers that need or only promises it.
- Lens #19, Needs. Which levels of Maslow's hierarchy does the game work on, how could it meet more basic needs, and how could it meet its current ones better? A game must deliver the need it promises or players leave (p.158).
- Judgment. The need to be judged is linked to self-esteem; people dislike only unfair judgment and work to be judged well, and games are good objective judges (p.158-159).
  - To apply, write what the target measures about the player, where the verdict appears, and whether a player would call it fair.
- Lens #20, Judgment. What does the game judge about players, how does it tell them, do they find it fair, do they care, and does it make them want to improve? (p.159)
- The designer's own modeling, focus, empathy and imagination are tools for understanding the player's; self-listening helps in listening to the audience (p.159).
