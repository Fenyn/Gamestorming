# Chapters 17 to 22: worlds, characters, spaces, aesthetics, other players, communities

Schell, The Art of Game Design, 1st edition. All page numbers are PDF pages (book page = PDF page - 31).
Paraphrased notes for a design agent. Read the book's own text with `book.py pages A B` before quoting.
Lenses 74 to 87 live in these chapters.

## Chapter 17. Stories and games take place in worlds (pp.330-338)

- Children with Star Wars action figures rarely replayed the film. They renamed the figures and invented new plots, so what drew them was the world, and the toys were a gateway into it that was interactive, portable and social (pp.331-332).
  To apply: list what a player of the target can do in its world that the main game never asks of them, and treat each item as a reason the world, not the plot, holds them.
- Transmedia worlds (Henry Jenkins's term) are fantasy worlds entered through many media. The product is the world, and each book, toy or game is sold as a gateway to part of it. Gateways that agree make the world feel more solid, and gateways that contradict each other make it collapse (p.332).
  To apply: put every gateway the project has (game, store page, card text, cast doc, trailer) side by side and log every fact where two of them disagree, then pick one version.
- Worlds feel more real than their media because people want them to exist, which is why a comic book is harder to throw away than a magazine (p.332).
- The Pokemon history: a Game Boy RPG with crude four-shade graphics but five years of balancing, then an anime that followed the game's rules and quest path closely enough to teach strategy, then a card game built on the same core mechanics that made play portable and social (pp.332-334).
  To apply: for each secondary product of the target (comic, card set, show, tie-in), check that its rules match the core game's rules, so that using it makes a player better at the game.
- The "binocular effect": the show gave players a vivid close-up of each Pokemon, which they then mapped onto the crude handheld sprites, the way opera glasses let a viewer fill in tiny figures on a stage (p.333).
  To apply: where the in-game art is small or low-detail, give one place (portrait, card art, cutscene) a detailed view of the same character so players carry that image back to the sprite.
- A world can be rooted in a game's ruleset and grow stronger with each medium added; the executive who expected the Pokemon movie to end the craze missed this (p.334).
- Properties of transmedia worlds. They are powerful, acting like a personal utopia fans visit for years, and the most devoted fan bases form around the strongest worlds (p.334). They are long lived, often passing from parents to children (p.335). They evolve as new gateways add features: Holmes's deerstalker and pipe came from the actor William Gillette, and Rudolph entered Santa's world in 1939 while Baum's Santa origin story failed. Visitors decide which additions stick, with no formal process (pp.335-336).
  To apply: when a fan, tester or later medium adds something to the world, record whether it took hold; do not defend an addition players ignore.
- What successful transmedia worlds share (pp.336-337):
  - They are rooted in a single medium where they first made a splash and where they stay strongest (p.336).
  - They are intuitive. Toontown in Roger Rabbit needed almost no description because everyone already assumed cartoon characters share a world (pp.336-337).
  - They have one creative individual at the core, or rarely a small tight team; large teams seldom produce one (p.337).
  - They support many stories, never one plotline, and leave room for future stories and for players' own (p.337).
  - They make sense through any gateway. "It makes more sense if you read the book" is a warning sign; Enter the Matrix and Animatrix confused anyone who had not seen the films (p.337).
  - They fulfil a wish. Imagining a world is work, so players only do it for a place they want to visit (p.337).
  To apply: score the target world against these six in writing, one sentence of evidence each, and for "any gateway" have a reader who has seen only one gateway explain the world back.
- Lens #74, The World. Ask how the world beats the real one, whether there can be several gateways and how they differ and support each other, and whether the world centres on one story or could hold many (p.338).

## Chapter 18. Worlds contain characters (pp.339-359)

- Comparing top novel, film and game characters shows three shifts as you move from novels to games: mental struggle becomes physical struggle, reality becomes fantasy, and complex becomes simple. Each medium shapes its characters. Simple physical characters are the easy path, and depth is possible but harder, as Final Fantasy VII shows (pp.341-342).
  To apply: for each main character, write the one conflict they face that is not physical; if none exists, note it as a gap.
- The avatar is the character the player controls. Players project into it the way drivers say "he hit me" about their car or bowlers lean as the ball rolls. A visible avatar becomes a "kinesthetic voodoo doll" whose blows make players wince (p.343).
- The ideal form is the first good avatar type: someone the player has wanted to be, such as a warrior, wizard or secret agent (p.343).
- The blank slate is the second type. Citing Scott McCloud, the less detail a character has, the easier it is to project into; alien or scary things get more detail to make them "other." An iconic character in a detailed world is a strong pairing (p.344). Mario is iconic; Spider-Man mixes both, an ideal form whose mask makes him iconic (p.345).
  To apply: rate the avatar's design on both axes, and if the protagonist is drawn in more detail than the world's monsters, consider simplifying the protagonist's face or the reverse.
- Warning: putting the player's own photo on the avatar stays a novelty because people play to be who they wish they were, not themselves (p.345).
- Lens #75, The Avatar. Ask whether the avatar is an ideal form that appeals to the audience and whether it has iconic qualities that let a player project into it (p.345).
- Character tip 1, list character functions. Write the functions the game needs (the platformer example: hero, mentor, assistant, tutor, final boss, minions, three bosses, hostage), then the characters already imagined (Princess Mouse, Wise Old Owl, Silver Hawk, Sammy Snake, Rat Army), and match them. Swap expected pairings, give one character several roles, and add characters only where a role is empty (pp.346-347).
  To apply: in the cast doc, make a two-column table of required functions and existing characters, draw the matches, mark doubled roles and empty roles, and try one unexpected pairing.
- Lens #76, Character Function. Ask what roles are needed, which characters exist, which map to which roles, whether any can fill several, whether characters must change to fit, and whether new ones are needed (p.347).
- Character tip 2, define and use character traits. A "character bible" listing everything is useful, but boil it down to a short list of traits that hold across many situations, and contradictions are allowed. The Sabu and Lester rewrite shows flat exposition turned into lines that each show two traits at once. Traits also belong in animation, such as a sneaky jump or a depressed character who walks instead of running (pp.347-348).
  To apply: give each named character three to five traits, then tag every line of one scene with the traits it shows and rewrite any line that shows none.
- Lens #77, Character Traits. Ask what traits define the character and how they show in words, actions and appearance (p.348).
- Character tip 3, the interpersonal circumplex. A social psychology graph with a friendly to hostile axis and a dominant to submissive axis, on which traits can be placed. Positions are relative, so put one character at the centre and plot the rest; the Han Solo plot shows Vader, Chewbacca and C-3PO at extremes, the closest companions nearest Han, and an empty lower-left quadrant (pp.348-350).
  To apply: plot the cast around the protagonist, then around the main villain, and note gaps, missing extremes and where friends sit.
- Lens #78, The Interpersonal Circumplex. Plot others around one character and ask whether gaps should be filled, whether extreme characters exist or should, and whether the character's friends share a quadrant and what changes if they did not (p.350).
- Character tip 4, make a character web. For each character, write what they think of every other character and why. The Archie, Veronica, Betty, Reggie and Jughead example runs every pair in both directions (pp.350-352).
  To apply: build an N by N grid in the cast doc, fill each cell with one sentence of how the row character feels about the column character, and flag empty cells and rows that read alike.
- Lens #79, The Character Web. Ask how each character feels about each other one, which connections are unused and how to use them, and which connections are too alike (p.352).
- Character tip 5, use status (from Keith Johnstone's Impro, introduced to Schell by Brenda Harger). Every interaction carries an unspoken negotiation of who is in charge, and status is something you do, not who you are, as the tramp who browbeats a duchess shows (pp.352-353).
  - Low status behaviours: fidgeting, avoiding eye contact, touching one's own face, general tension (p.353).
  - High status behaviours: being relaxed and in control, strong eye contact, keeping the head still while speaking (p.353).
  - The improv exercise where one group breaks eye contact and the other holds it makes actors feel inferior or superior within minutes (p.353).
  - Status is relative: Vader is high with Leia and low with the Emperor. Slow motion conveys high status. Low-status characters keep to places where they will not be noticed, and high-status characters take the most important spot in the room (pp.353-354).
  - Most game characters act the same whoever is nearby. Giving them status behaviours makes them seem aware of each other; Munch's Oddysee gets comedy from status reversals, and arguments are interesting as a seesaw of status (p.354).
  To apply: for each scene or NPC pairing, write who is high and who is low, then set posture, eye contact, head movement and position in the room to match, and mark the moment status flips.
- Lens #80, Status. Ask the relative status of the characters, how they can show it, how they compete for it, where status changes happen, and how the player gets to express status (pp.354-355).
- Character tip 6, the power of the voice. Voice works on people below conscious thought. Games do voice backwards: animation writes the script, records actors who improvise, then designs the character around the recording, while games model and animate first and record last, so the actor only imitates what is on screen. The cause is scripts that keep changing (p.355).
  To apply: if voice or barks are planned, record or temp-record a performance before final portraits and animations are locked, and adjust the character to the performance.
- Character tip 7, the power of the face. Much of the brain processes faces; people alone show eye whites, blushing and crying. OnLive Traveler testers asked for more facial detail each round until the bodies were cut to floating heads. Cheap eyebrow or eye-shape animation goes a long way. Doom put the avatar's face on the HUD and tied its expression to health, because peripheral vision reads a face faster than a number (pp.355-357).
  To apply: find one place where the player watches a number to judge a character's state and test a face or expression that carries the same information.
- Character tip 8, powerful stories transform characters. Fixed types (born hero, permanent villain) make dull stories; Fable and Knights of the Old Republic stood out because events changed the lead. If the avatar cannot change, a sidekick or villain can. Make a character transformation chart with characters down the side and story sections across the top, and fill each cell with the character's state; the Cinderella chart is the example (pp.357-358).
  To apply: build that chart for the cast against the acts or floors of the target and highlight rows that never change.
- Lens #81, Character Transformation. Ask how each character changes, how the change is shown to the player and whether it could be clearer or stronger, whether there is enough change, and whether it is surprising, interesting and believable (p.358).
- Character tip 9, avoid the uncanny valley. Masahiro Mori observed that empathy rises with human likeness (rock, teddy bear, puppy, person) until near-human figures such as a robot with skin turn repulsive, perhaps because they read as diseased people. Zombies sit at the bottom. CG humans in Final Fantasy and Polar Express look fine in a still and wrong in motion, while Pixar's cartoon fish, toys and cars stay on the safe left side (pp.358-359).
  To apply: when a character is meant to be liked, push its style toward the stylised side; review it in motion, not from a still.
- The book does not give a separate tip on using the circumplex and the web together.

## Chapter 19. Worlds contain spaces (pp.360-374)

- Functional space from chapter 10 is the skeleton; this chapter covers the fleshed-out space the player experiences (p.361).
- The primary purpose of architecture is to control a person's experience. A building is judged well designed by how it feels inside, not how its shape looks. Architects and game designers both build structures people enter, both work only through indirect control, and both exist to produce experiences (p.361).
  To apply: for each location, write the experience it should produce in one line, then judge the layout only against that line.
- Virtual spaces lack physical constraints, which is a freedom and a burden. Start from an organizing principle, ideally the functional space from Lens #21 (pp.361-362).
- Five ways to organize a game space (pp.362-364):
  - Linear, forward and maybe back along a line that may loop (Candyland, Monopoly, Super Mario Bros., Guitar Hero) (p.362).
  - Grid, of squares, rectangles, hexes or triangles; easy to read, keeps proportion, easy for computers (Chess, Advance Wars, Catan, NES Zelda, Quake) (pp.362-363).
  - Web, points joined by paths so players have several routes to the places they must visit; travel may be meaningful or instant (Trivial Pursuit, Zork, Club Penguin, Toontown) (p.363).
  - Points in space, wandering between scattered spots like returning to an oasis, or spaces players define themselves (Bocce, Final Fantasy) (pp.363-364).
  - Divided space, a map carved into irregular regions (Risk, Axis and Allies, Ocarina of Time, Spore) (p.364).
  - Combinations: Clue mixes grid and divided space; baseball mixes linear and points in space (p.364).
  To apply: name which of the five a floor or hub uses, draw its skeleton in that form, and check the choice against how the player is meant to move.
- Landmarks. Colossal Cave had a maze of passages "all alike" and one "all different," and both confused equally because too much chaos is as monotonous as too much order; players dropped items to make their own landmarks. Landmarks guide players, make a space worth looking at, and are what players remember and talk about (pp.364-365).
  To apply: mark every landmark on the layout and check that a player at any spot can see or recall one; if testers drop markers or ask where they are, add one there.
- Christopher Alexander's nameless quality (The Timeless Way of Building, with the tea, reading light and cushions passage). Things with it feel alive, whole, comfortable, free, exact, egoless and eternal, and are free from inner contradictions (p.365).
- Inner contradictions are at the heart of bad design: a device meant to make life easier that is hard to use, or something meant to be fun that bores. Remove them and do not get used to them (pp.365-366).
- Lens #82, Inner Contradiction. Ask the purpose of the game and of each subsystem, whether anything contradicts those purposes, and how to change it (p.366).
  To apply: write one purpose line per subsystem, then list every rule, UI element and content piece that works against a line.
- Alexander holds that good design comes only from iteration and watching use, the Rule of the Loop in architecture: plant grass, wait a year to see where people wear paths, then pave (p.366).
  To apply: ship a hub or floor with minimal fixed routes, log where testers walk, and place paths, shortcuts and shops on those routes.
- A Pattern Language lists 253 patterns from town scale down to "windows which open wide." It shaped Toontown's world, reportedly Sim City, and the software design patterns movement (p.366).
- The Phenomenon of Life distils fifteen properties shared by things with the nameless quality, which echo the qualities of living things (pp.366-367).
- Alexander's fifteen properties of living structures, as Schell applies them to games (pp.367-368):
  1. Levels of scale: telescoping short, mid and long-term goals, fractal interest curves, nested world structure; Spore is the example (p.367).
  2. Strong centers: in layout and story; a strong avatar at the centre and a strong goal (p.367).
  3. Boundaries: territory games are about them, and rules are boundaries too (p.367).
  4. Alternating repetition: the chessboard, level then boss then level, tension then release (p.367).
  5. Positive space: foreground and background with complementary shapes; in games, alternative strategies that interlock (p.367).
  6. Good shape: pleasing shapes in visuals, and a level that feels solid with a "good curve" (p.367).
  7. Local symmetries: many small internal symmetries instead of one mirror image, as in Wind Waker rooms joined organically; also rules and balance (p.367).
  8. Deep interlock and ambiguity: two things defining each other, as Go pieces mean something only relative to the opponent's (p.367).
  9. Contrast: between opponents, controllable and uncontrollable, reward and punishment (p.368).
  10. Gradients: gradual change, such as a rising challenge curve or well-designed probability curves (p.368).
  11. Roughness: a game too perfect has no character; house rules make it feel alive (p.368).
  12. Echoes: unifying repetition, such as a boss sharing features with its minions, or fractal interest curves (p.368).
  13. The void: a calm empty heart surrounded by clutter, as in a church, or a boss in a large hollow room (p.368).
  14. Simplicity and inner calm: few rules with emergent results, balanced so they feel calm (p.368).
  15. Not-separateness: every rule and element connected to its surroundings, giving the whole game a living feel (p.368).
  To apply: go down the fifteen for one floor, hub or system, write yes or no with the element that shows it, and pick two missing properties to try in the next iteration.
- Lens #83, The Nameless Quality. Ask whether the design feels alive or has dead parts and what would make it more alive, which of the fifteen qualities it has and how it could have more, and where the design feels like the designer's own self (pp.368-369).
- Real vs. virtual architecture. Game spaces often have wasted space, dangerous features, no link to their outside, and even overlap themselves impossibly, yet players do not notice, because people store spaces as relations (which door leads where), not as 2D maps. Only how the space feels from inside matters (pp.369-370).
  To apply: judge a level by walking it at play camera, not from the top-down editor view, and do not fix impossible geometry the player cannot detect.
- Know how big. Virtual spaces lack scale cues (light, shadow, texture, stereo vision, one's own body), so things end up out of scale. Use a unit you know physically, feet or meters, so a 30-unit car is obviously wrong (p.370).
  - Eye height: a first-person camera above about seven feet or below five feet distorts the world (p.370).
  - People and doorways are the strongest scale cues; giants, little people or odd doorways confuse scale, and a space with no people or standard man-made objects leaves scale unclear (pp.370-371).
  - Texture scaling: bricks or tiles at the wrong size break scale; match real-world texture sizes (p.371).
  To apply: fix one world unit to a real measure, place a person-sized figure and a door in every room or tile set, and compare brick, tile and furniture sizes against them.
- Third-person distortion. Seeing the avatar from behind makes normal-sized interiors feel cramped, like driving a car around a house, and players blame the room (p.371). Fixes tried (pp.372-373): scale up room and furniture (avatar feels like a small child); scale up the room only (cavernous, furniture huddled); scale up the room and spread normal furniture (sparse); scale up the room, enlarge furniture a little and spread it out, the Max Payne solution, which looks odd in first person but right in third.
  To apply: for third-person or high-angle interiors, enlarge rooms, enlarge furniture slightly, widen gaps between props, and test at play camera.
- Level design is game design done in detail: arranging architecture, props and challenges for the right challenge, reward and meaningful choice. It differs per game, and the way through is to look at the level through many lenses (p.374).
  To apply: run a level through the same lenses used on the whole game (challenge, reward, choice, interest curve) before shipping it.

## Chapter 20. The look and feel of a world is defined by its aesthetics (pp.376-383)

- Aesthetics is the third element of the tetrad, and dismissing it as "surface" forgets that the design is a whole experience (p.378).
- What good artwork does for a game (p.378): it draws in players who would have passed the game over; it makes the world feel solid and real, so players take it more seriously and endogenous value rises (the Axis and Allies story from chapter 8); every new thing seen becomes a reward; and players forgive design flaws behind a beautiful surface.
  To apply: list what the player sees for the first time on each floor or screen and check that each new sight counts as a reward; flag reused art in places meant to feel like progress.
- Reuse older lenses on the art instead of the mechanics. The book lists Lenses 1, 2, 4, 9, 10, 11, 15, 16, 17, 40, 42, 43, 45, 48, 49, 54, 55, 59, 64, 67, 72, 74, 75, 80, 82 and 83, plus #63 Beauty (pp.378-379).
  To apply: pick three of these and answer their questions about the target's artwork only.
- Learning to see. The brain labels objects ("salt shaker") instead of seeing shape, colour, proportion, shadow, texture, context, function and meaning. Train the right brain to see; Betty Edwards's Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain teaches this, and drawing and seeing improve each other (pp.379-380).
  To apply: before judging a sprite or scene, describe its shapes, colours and shadows without naming objects, then compare that description to the reference.
- Let aesthetics guide design. Bring artists in early: a sketch can change a design's course, give the uniting vision, show whether an interface idea works, or a joke doodle can become the theme. Designs are abstract and illustrations concrete (p.380).
- Designers with a distinct visual style (Miyamoto, American McGee) are one of the two kinds that get famous, so a designer with a strong style should build games around it; a designer without drawing skill should find an artist partner (pp.380-381).
- Good concept art for a strong design makes the idea clear to everyone, lets people see and imagine entering the world, makes people want to play it, makes people want to work on it, and helps secure funding (p.381).
- Concept art is another kind of prototype. Alternate abstract prototypes and concept art like a seesaw so the art and the play grow up together (p.381).
  To apply: after each playable prototype pass, make one concept image of the moment it is about; after each image, ask what play change it suggests.
- How much is enough. Rough sketches are often enough, and making rough useful sketches is a skill young artists fear to show. One artist's trick: a large rough pencil sketch with one corner rendered to finished quality, showing both scope and finish (pp.381-382).
  To apply: when presenting a mockup, render one small region to final quality and leave the rest rough.
- Place detail with judgement, because a few details in the right spots make a world feel bigger. John Hench: looking good from far away is easy, looking good up close is hard; Cinderella Castle has mosaics and stonework close up (p.382).
  To apply: identify the spots where the player stops close to things (shops, dialogue, chests) and spend detail there first.
- Tolkien's "distant mountains": name places, people and events that are never visited so the world feels larger, and do not detail them, or you need further distant mountains behind them (p.382).
  To apply: in dialogue, item text and maps, add a few named places and events the game never shows, and keep a list so later text stays consistent with them.
- Use audio. Audio feedback is more visceral than visual and stands in for touch. In one study, players with better audio rated identical graphics higher (p.382).
- Kyle Gabler's technique: choose the game's music at the very start, even before the game is defined. If part of the game conflicts with music that feels right, that part should change (pp.382-383).
  To apply: pick a reference track for the target now and, at each review, note any screen or mechanic that clashes with it.
- Balancing art and technology. Let artists drive the aesthetic vision and make engineers the navigators and mechanics who suggest routes and improve the bus; have artists paint the shadows they want and challenge engineers to match. Hire a technical artist who speaks both languages and builds tools; when the balance is wrong the game feels cracked down the middle (p.383).
  To apply: for a rendering or shader feature, get the target look as a painted or mocked frame first, then build the tech to match it.
- The art bible appears later, in chapter 24's list of game documents. It is a guideline document (character sheets, environment examples, colour use, interface examples) that keeps several artists on one consistent look; concept art overview images sit alongside it (p.416).
  To apply: collect the locked palette, one approved character sheet, one environment and one UI example into one document before a second artist or agent draws for the project.
- Chapter 20 defines no new lens.

## Chapter 21. Some games are played with other players (pp.384-387)

- Most games in history are social, and solo games were rare before computers. The run of single-player videogames came from novelty and technical limits, and games are moving back toward social molds, though solitary play will remain (p.385).
- Five reasons people play with others (pp.386-387):
  1. Competition: a level field (Lens #30), a worthy opponent (#31, #36), a problem to solve (#6), measuring skill against one's social circle (#20, #80), and complex strategy and psychology made possible by a human opponent (#32, #27, #71) (p.386).
  2. Collaboration: actions and strategies one person cannot do, and the pleasure of group problem solving and being on a winning team. Most collaborative games are team sports, combining collaboration and competition (p.386).
  3. Meeting up: games, like food, give friends a reason to gather and something to focus on that avoids awkwardness (p.386).
  4. Exploring our friends: play shows how friends solve problems, decide under stress, when they cut someone a break or betray them, and who can be trusted. Plato's line about an hour of play is quoted (pp.386-387).
  5. Exploring ourselves: how we act under social stress, whether we let friends win, how we take public defeat, whom we copy (p.387).
  To apply: for an online mode, write which of the five reasons it serves and point to the feature that serves each; drop the mode or redesign if none is clear.
- Warning: assume a multiplayer online game costs about four times a similar single-player game, because it is harder to debug and balance. "Because multiplay is cool" is not a reason (p.387).
- Chapter 21 defines no new lens.

## Chapter 22. Other players sometimes form communities (pp.388-401)

- Communities of fans, players or designers extend a game's life by years (p.389).
- Four elements of a sense of community, from two unnamed psychologists: membership, influence, integration and fulfilment of needs, and shared emotional connection (p.389). Amy Jo Kim's shorter definition: people with a shared interest, purpose or goal who get to know each other over time (p.389).
- Three reasons to want a community (pp.389-390): it meets a social need (Lens #19); it lengthens the "period of contagion," the time a player talks about the game constantly, and Will Wright notes that doubling it can multiply spread by ten; and it adds hours of play, as in the family card game with almost no decisions that they played for hours because it kept them at the table together.
- Ten tips for strong communities (pp.390-398):
  - Tip 1, foster friendships. A friendship online needs the ability to talk freely, someone worth talking to (which depends on the audience: adults want people who share their problems, teenagers want new and interesting people, children want their real-life friends), and something worth talking about, from strategy depth, events or rule changes. Good online games balance community and game (pp.390-391). Friendship has three phases: breaking the ice through low-pressure ways to meet and show oneself, becoming friends through talk about a shared play experience plus a ritual such as a friends list, and staying friends through ways to find each other again such as friends lists, guilds or memorable names (pp.391-392).
    To apply: for each phase, name the exact screen or feature in the online mode that handles it, and list phases with nothing.
  - Lens #84, Friendship. Ask what friendships the players want, how they break the ice, whether they can talk enough and have enough to talk about, when they become friends, and what tools help them keep the friendship (p.392).
  - Tip 2, put conflict at the heart (Jonathan Baron). Communities form around conflict. The game's conflict must push players to prove they are best, or be easier to resolve together; card games do both, competition plus shared strategy talk (pp.392-393).
    To apply: state which kind of conflict the online mode has and what players will discuss about it outside matches.
  - Tip 3, use architecture to shape the community. Walkable neighbourhoods with places to go and dead-end streets let people see the same faces often. Online, build places on the way to important business where players pass repeatedly and have time to talk (p.393).
    To apply: in the hub, put the social spot on the route between the most-used services, not off to one side.
  - Tip 4, create community property: things owned by a group, such as a ship no one player can afford, or a guild's status (p.393).
  - Tip 5, let players express themselves through avatar creation, emotive chat, text colour and style. Offline examples: Charades, Pictionary, Shawn Patton's game of colouring dirt onto your character, and Monopoly's twelve tokens for at most eight players (pp.393-394).
    To apply: list every player-chosen element visible to others (name, avatar, frame, title, deck back) and add one where others can see only defaults.
  - Lens #85, Expression. Ask how players express themselves, which ways are forgotten, and whether players are proud of their identity and why; it pairs with Lens #63 Beauty and #80 Status (p.394).
  - Tip 6, support three levels. The newbie, for whom learning is the game, so learning must be rewarding, ideally through meaningful contact with experienced players (an online Battletech had generals recruit newbies for the front line) (pp.394-395). The player, who gets most of the design (p.395). The elder, who has used up the game and will leave unless given a different game: a harder game (Toontown's Cog Headquarters), governance privileges (MUD rule-setting, card game judges), the joy of creation (The Sims, Unreal Tournament content), guild management tools, or a chance to teach with outfits marking teachers (pp.395-396). The neighbourhood Easter egg hunt shows the three levels: eggs in plain sight for ages 2 to 5, a normal hunt for 6 to 9, and the oldest kids hiding the eggs (p.396).
    To apply: write what a first-week, a regular and a veteran player each do in the mode, and if the veteran row reads "the same as regular," pick one elder game from the five.
  - Tip 7, force players to depend on each other. A game fully masterable solo lowers the value of community. Taking something away can help: in Toontown players cannot heal themselves in battle, only others, which made them ask for and give help (pp.396-397).
    To apply: find one ability the player gives themselves and test making it something only another player can give.
  - Tip 8, manage your community. Build tools for players to talk and organise, and consider community managers who act like gardeners and keep a feedback loop between players and designers; Amy Jo Kim's Community Building on the Web covers the hands-on and hands-off balance (p.397).
  - Tip 9, obligation to others is powerful. Promises ("meet at 10 Wednesday") and owed favours are taken seriously; guild obligation keeps World of Warcraft players returning, partly to avoid the low status of breaking commitments (pp.397-398).
    To apply: add one way for players to commit to a time or owe a favour, and make the commitment visible to the other party.
  - Tip 10, create community events. Events give something to look forward to, create shared experience, punctuate time, guarantee a chance to meet others, and make players check back for the next one; a goal plus a mass email can be enough (p.398).
    To apply: put a recurring event on a calendar for the online mode with a fixed day and a stated goal.
- Lens #86, Community. Ask what conflict sits at the heart of the community, how architecture shapes it, whether the game supports three levels of experience, whether there are events, and why players need each other (p.398).
- Griefing. Some players enjoy tormenting others more than the game; they are Bartle's Joker and see themselves as high status because they can spoil what others care about (p.399). Banning griefers forces you to police and judge intent, so it is better to avoid systems that make griefing easy (p.399):
  - Player vs. player combat outside a PvP-core game makes players feel unsafe and afraid to talk to strangers; the befriend-then-kill-and-loot trick is the example. Confine PvP to special areas or circumstances (p.399).
  - Stealing, including pickpocketing, looting and kill stealing (early EverQuest gave all XP to the final blow). Make it hard to take what is not yours (pp.399-400).
  - Trading where items can be misrepresented. Give full information about traded items (p.400).
  - Obscenities. Black and white lists get beaten; combine an automated filter with player reporting, and give no feedback: let the griefer see their own message unfiltered while others see it filtered (p.400).
  - Blocking the way. Let players slip past each other, make doorways too wide for one player to block, or let players push; Toontown chose pushing, and griefers then pushed idle avatars into battles (p.400).
  - Loopholes such as disconnecting to deny loot, crashing servers, or arranging furniture into obscene words. Watch for them and remove them as they appear (p.400).
  To apply: walk the online mode's feature list against these six categories and, for each hit, note the specific abuse and the design change that removes the payoff.
- Lens #87, Griefing. Ask which systems are easy to grief, how to make griefing boring, and which loopholes are being ignored (p.401).
- The future of communities. Online identity is personal and long-lived; many people keep a handle for decades and choosing one is a rite of passage. Schell imagines childhood avatars and guilds that last a lifetime like loyalty to a sports team, and avatars memorialised or passed to descendants (p.401).
  To apply: treat player names, tags and profile history as permanent; avoid forced renames or resets and give players a way to keep their identity across versions.
