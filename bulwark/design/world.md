# Bulwark — The World

## The shape of things

This is a post-war world. The fighting ended a generation ago, but the scars have not healed
and the peace has not settled into anything comfortable. Aurenthal and the elven nations
fought, neither side won, and neither side can claim the moral high ground. Both stopped
because neither had the strength to continue, and Aurenthal has been contracting ever since.

The frontier is where the contraction shows first. The garrisons withdrew, the roads went
unrepaired, and the chapels lost their clergy. The people who could leave did. The ones who
stayed are stubborn or have nowhere else to go. The outpost the player is sent to reinforce is
one of the last surviving structures from an era when the crown still cared about what lay
beyond the capital walls.

The world runs on the tension between what people build and what time reclaims: stone against
rot, hearth against cold, dawn against shadow, the cycle of death and regrowth. Every deity,
every culture, every character in the cast is positioned somewhere along that axis.

---

## Aurenthal and the Empty Throne

Aurenthal is the hegemony that unified the human kingdoms under a single crown. At its height,
under King Lanric, it was vast and prosperous. Trade routes ran deep into the frontier.
Aveline's chapels rose in every settlement. The dwarves built the infrastructure: roads, walls,
bridges, fortifications. The humans filled them with commerce, governance, and ambition.

Lanric reigned through all of it: the height, the long war with the elven nations, and the last
day of both. He died at Ilvaren, standing on ground his army had taken, when the goddess of the
captured shrine became the blast that ended the war. The golden age and the war ended in the
same hour.

He left no heir. The throne has sat empty for a generation while the great houses of the
capital maneuver for it, openly and otherwise, and the balance among them shifts too often for
any claim to settle. The ministries govern as caretakers in the meantime, and a caretaker
government maintains what it has rather than building anything new. The frontier sends
requisitions; clerks weigh them against nearer needs, and the answer is usually no.

From inside the capital the contraction is hard to see. The city still looks like the songs,
and the songs are what the army recruits on, for it has nothing newer to offer: Lanric's
garrisoned frontier, chapels in every hamlet, dwarven roads running to the edge of the map.
Every word of it was true a generation ago. The academy that trains the crown's soldiers and
wizards admits on merit, but postings flow through house patronage. A cadet with a house behind
him takes a capital garrison; a cadet with no name takes whatever is left.

The crown is diminished, and it is not a villain: a government that makes sensible decisions
in marble halls and does not look too closely at what those decisions cost the people who
live beyond them.

## The War and the Ashfall

The conflict between Aurenthal and the elven nations was fought over territory, resources,
and the usual accumulation of grievances that builds when two civilizations share a continent
and disagree about whose claim to it runs deeper.

The war was long enough to become normal and ugly enough to leave marks that will not fade in
a single lifetime. Both sides did things they will not speak of easily. Aurenthal's soldiers
followed orders that should not have been given. The dwarves, as always, served as
infrastructure: engineers, builders, fortifiers. They raised the siege works and the supply
lines and the walls, and when the walls came down they were standing behind them.

It ended at Ilvaren. Ilvaren was an elven shrine-town at a river ford, its shrine raised to
Ilva, the protector-goddess of the elven pantheon. The ford made it a prize: whoever held the
crossing held the road into the elven interior. Aurenthal's army took the town, and Lanric
came in person to claim what his soldiers had won. Then the shrine went. A shrine is a channel
to its god, and something drove Ilva through it all at once: the goddess herself was the
blast, and the shrine only the point where she entered the world. It annihilated everything
within its radius: the army, the king, the town, and Ilva. Survivors at the edge of it
absorbed divine energy that minds were never meant to hold.

What triggered it is the war's last and worst dispute. Aurenthal holds that the elven command
detonated their own goddess rather than yield the crossing, spite dressed as strategy. The
elves hold that human hands in the captured shrine broke the channel open, through sabotage or
desecration or plain ignorant greed. No one close enough to know survived. Accounts of that
day disagree on almost every detail except the outcome, and it cannot happen twice. There is
no second Ilva.

Aurenthal names the whole war for its last day: the Ashfall. The elves name the place. To an
elf the war is Ilvaren, and the town's name now carries all of it. Most humans do not know the
elven name.

No treaty followed. Aurenthal had no sovereign left to sign one, and each side believes the
other killed Ilva; neither will sit across a table from that. The elves keep the cold peace
in part because their shield is gone. Trade has resumed and diplomacy functions, both barely.
Trust does not exist.

Elara was born in Ilvaren. She was away on a trading run when it died, having spent the war
years supplying the elven armies, and she came home to the crater. Her profile
(`characters/elara.md`) carries the rest.

## The Frontier

The frontier is the empire's edge, the borderlands where civilization thins and the wild
begins. Once it was garrisoned, patrolled, and maintained: chapels of Aveline in every hamlet,
dwarven-built roads between the outposts, farmers on the soil and traders on the routes. It was
never the heartland, but it was held.

It is not held now. The garrisons withdrew, the farmers left, and the roads cracked. The
chapels stand empty or half-ruined, tended only by the faithful few who refused to abandon
them. The landscape is reclaiming itself, overgrown and hollowed out, returning to what it was
before the crown built on it.

Where the old infrastructure has failed outright — a bridge down, a cutting flooded — no crews
have come. Travelers wear detour tracks around the wreckage instead: paths on no map, made by
feet and maintained by feet, and by now some of the detours are older than the maps that ignore
them.

The border itself has receded. This ground was interior once, well inside the realm's reach, but
the crown's living hold has drawn inward with the contraction, one abandoned stretch at a time.
The maps and the schools have not kept pace. The academy still teaches the old extent as settled
fact, and crown paper still claims the wider realm, so those schooled on it carry a kingdom in
their heads larger than the one that still answers. A traveler taught from the old records can
pass the living border without knowing they have crossed anything, and read the decay around them
as neglect rather than the frontier it has quietly become.

### The Elderwood

The great forest that borders the outpost. It has many names along the frontier, **Thornwood**
near the outpost and **Elderwood** in the deeper reaches, but it is one continuous forest,
older than the hegemony and older than the wars. The trees remember a time before the roads,
and they have been waiting patiently for the roads to leave.

**Resolved (2026-07-16):** Thornwood and Elderwood are the near and deep reaches of a single forest,
not two forests. The **Thornwood is the near forest**, the game's starting territory, the **Verdant
Fringe**, safe enough to gather and cut timber in. The **Elderwood is the deep reaches**, older and
darker, and it is gated: a **dire wolf has come down out of the deep and guards the passage between the
two**. Until the wolf is slain, the party cannot press past the Thornwood into the Elderwood. Killing it
is what opens the deeper forest (mechanically, the `dire_wolf_slain` flag unlocks the Elderwood
territory). Everything the Elderwood supplies (hardwood, coal, its own forage and beasts, and
the outsiders who live there like Spore and Thistle) sits behind that kill.

The forest is indifferent, not malicious. It does not hate the outpost; it does not recognize
the outpost's claim to the ground. With the garrison gone from the boundary, the forest has
pressed inward season by season, reclaiming what was cleared.

Within the deep Elderwood, older things stir. Grandmother Mulch has been tending the cycle of
rot and regrowth since before any tree currently standing was a seed. Fey connections flicker
at the edges: doors that open and close at their own pleasure, leaving gnomes stranded on the
wrong side. Leshies grow from the forest floor, animated by spirits older than memory.

The outpost stands here because this is where civilization's line was drawn. It is said the
forest's edge nearby was once a border, negotiated or imposed, between the settled world and
what predates settlement entirely. The garrison that held that line is gone. The outpost,
whether the player knows it yet or not, is rebuilding the bulwark.

### What presses at the edges

The forest grows bolder because the line is no longer held. There is no single named threat
behind it, only the accumulated pressure of a wild place that was held at arm's length for
generations and is now testing whether the arm is still there. Creatures venture closer, the
undergrowth advances, and the old paths grow over. An empty border invites what once kept its
distance.

The pressure is ecological, not military. The outpost's job, and the player's, is to
re-establish the balance: to hold the line firmly enough that both sides of it can exist, not
to conquer the forest.

## The Age of Shadow and the Dawn

Before the hegemony, before the crown, before the roads and the walls and the chapels, the
world was dark. The faith holds that this was literal: the long shadow hung over everything,
the dead walked freely, and the living huddled where they could.

Aveline set her hand against the horizon and the sun came, and with it warmth and the slow
green mending of all wounded things. This is the foundational myth of the hegemony's faith, and
it credits the Lady of Dawn with the world itself, not only with healing. Her chapels, now
half-ruined along the frontier, are monuments to the idea that the darkness was pushed back
once and can be pushed back again.

Whether the Age of Shadow is literal history or theological metaphor depends on who you ask.
The faithful (Tharr, Aldric, Sister Cael) treat it as fact. The scholars debate. The practical,
the player and Elara among them, note that the chapels exist and the darkness they were built
against has not entirely gone away.

## The Divine

The gods are present, and concretely so. Their power flows through clerics and champions and
oracles who did not ask for it, their chapels are built of real stone by real hands, and their
edicts and anathema carry real consequences.

### Aveline, the Lady of Dawn
The first light. Compassion expressed through action. Her faithful carry lanterns into dark
places and offer the open hand before the closed fist. Her chapels ran through the hegemony
like a golden thread during Lanric's reign. Now they stand half-ruined along the frontier,
tended by those who will not leave. Aldric knelt in one and rose as a Champion. Tharr's outpost
once had one. It can again.

### Valdûr, the Stonefather
The earth itself. His body is the world: mountains his spine, veins of ore his blood. Dwarves
believe they were carved from his flesh and owe a debt of craft in return. Prayer is done with
a hammer in hand. Temples are workshops. A perfectly laid wall is a hymn. Tharr's faith is
Valdûr's faith: the quality of your work reflects the quality of your soul. "If the stones
hold, the Stonefather approves."

### Lyndra, the Hearthkeeper
The halfling goddess who showed up, sat down, and never left. Kinship, the hearth, the shared
meal. Her faith has no formal clergy; any halfling who cooks for others is performing a rite.
Fenwick's kitchen IS his temple, and every meal is an offering. "A full belly and a warm fire.
What else does anyone need?"

### Ilva, the Ward (dead)
Protector-goddess of the elven pantheon: shields, fords, gates, the holding of lines. Her
shrines stood wherever ground needed keeping, which is why one stood at Ilvaren. The elven
pantheon is broad and Ilva stood high in it, though not at its head. Her death maimed the
faith; it did not end it. Her clerics felt her go, mid-prayer, in every elven city at once.
Elves do not speak her name casually now. See `deities/ilva.md`.

### Grandmother Mulch
A patron, not a deity: the oldest thing in the forest, the process by which the forest digests
itself. Rot feeds the root and decay becomes growth. She is kind on the forest's timescale, not
a mortal one. She opposes waste, not civilization. Spore is her vessel and her witch, doing the
small close work that Grandmother Mulch is too large to do herself.

## The Prose Voice

Bulwark's dialogue and lore use an elevated register: old words over modern ones, formal sentence
shapes, and oaths sworn by what each speaker believes in. It is cultural, not decorative. These
people were raised on songs of the golden age and swear by the gods whose chapels they grew up in,
and the formality is the water they swim in. A commoner on the frontier is archaic in vocabulary
and quick with contractions; the crown is formal in syntax and uses none. Station lives in that
difference.

The full writing manual — the dictionary, the per-character register, the chronicle voice for lore
entries, and the forbidden-construction checklist — lives in its own document, **`prose_style.md`**.
Read that before authoring any dialogue or lore text.

## Key tensions (thematic, not mechanical)

- **Building vs. decay**: the outpost rises while the forest presses. Neither is wrong.
- **Memory vs. present**: the hegemony remembers its golden age. The frontier lives in its
  aftermath. The gap between what was and what is drives every character differently.
- **Shame vs. purpose**: the wars left scars on every faction. The elves, the hegemony, the
  dwarves who built the machinery of it. The outpost is a restoration project and also a chance
  to build something that is not defined by what came before.
- **The line**: civilization and the wild need a boundary: a border, not a war or a conquest.
  The outpost is that border. The bulwark.
