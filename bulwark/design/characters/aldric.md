# Sir Aldric

- **id:** `aldric`
- **Class:** Champion (Paladin cause)
- **Ancestry:** Human
- **Pronouns:** he/him
- **Kind:** recruitable PC
- **Associated building:** Training Yard
- **Deity:** Aveline, the Lady of Dawn (see `../deities/aveline.md`)

## Vibe

Aldric is formal and measured. He speaks the way a man speaks when he has learned the cost of
careless words. There is a steadiness to him that people mistake for calm, but it is closer to
discipline, the kind that comes from someone who once lost control and has spent every day since
making sure it does not happen again.

He is courteous without warmth, at first. The courtesy is genuine but practiced, a wall built
from good manners. Underneath it is a man who is terrified of being the person he used to be.
As he settles into the outpost and earns trust, the wall comes down in small ways. He laughs
more. He offers advice instead of waiting to be asked. He trains the others not because it is
his duty but because he does not want them to face what he faced without being ready.

## Role in the town

Aldric runs the Training Yard. He drills the outpost's fighters, oversees sparring, and handles
proficiency training and respeccing. As the Training Yard upgrades, the range of martial
instruction he can offer expands. He is a skilled teacher who understands combat from both
sides of a war, and that perspective makes his training sharper than most.

In the field, he is a defensive anchor. Champions in Pathfinder are built around protecting
allies, and Aldric's Paladin cause gives him retributive strike, punishing enemies who harm
those he has sworn to defend. He fights like a man who has decided that no one else dies on
his watch.

## Background

Aldric was a soldier. A good one, by the metrics that soldiers are measured by. He followed
orders, held the line, and did what was asked of him. The problem was what was asked of him.

The details of which wars and which deeds haunt him are his to share or not, and he does not
share them easily. What is known is that he left the army, surrendered his commission, and
walked into a chapel of Aveline with his sword across his palms. He knelt and did not rise for
three days. When he stood, he was different. Not healed. Different.

He took up Aveline's cause because her edicts are the exact inverse of what he was ordered to
do. Destroy the undead, not the living. Bring light to dark places, not fire. Offer mercy to
those who surrender. He follows her teachings with the fervor of a man who has seen what happens
when mercy is absent.

He heard about the outpost through travelers. A settlement growing on the frontier, building
something from nothing, protecting the borderlands. It sounded like the kind of place where a
man could do more good than harm. He arrived with his armor polished and his vows intact,
offered his sword and his training, and asked for nothing in return except a place to be useful.

## Arrival

Recruitable. Arrives voluntarily after hearing of the outpost's growth. The trigger is eight
buildings constructed at the outpost, a visible sign of the growth word would actually travel on,
and reason enough for a soldier to believe the place is worth committing his sword to.

## Notes (deferred / later)

- Build: Champion (Paladin cause) + Bastion FA line. Longsword and steel shield, heavy armor.
  Cha 18 or Str 18 (Champions key off Cha for class DC but want Str for melee). Paladin cause
  grants Retributive Strike (reaction to punish enemies who damage allies).
- Champion is listed as planned in the engine (no feature-class yet). Champion reactions,
  divine ally, and cause-specific abilities will need implementation.
- The "Sir" title suggests knighthood or equivalent rank from his former life. This could be
  a source of tension if anyone from his past recognizes him.
- His arc is atonement, not redemption. He does not believe he can undo what he did. He
  believes he can make sure it was the last time.
- Aveline's edicts ("destroy the undead, bring light to dark places, offer mercy to those who
  surrender") function as both his religious obligations and his personal rules. Breaking any
  of them should carry narrative weight.
