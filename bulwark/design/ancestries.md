# Bulwark — Ancestry Lore

How the peoples of the world relate to each other and to the dominant human hegemony.

## Humans — The Hegemony

One enormous kingdom that expanded until it ran out of things worth conquering. At its height
it resembled Rome: a single political engine that absorbed, administered, and then forgot about
the places it claimed. The frontier outpost was built during that expansion, garrisoned, supplied,
and then quietly deprioritized as the empire's attention turned inward. The people at the edges
are still technically subjects of the crown. Nobody at the crown remembers they exist.

Most humans in the world live inside the hegemony's borders and think of it the way fish think
of water. It is the default. The kingdom is not cruel by design, but it is large enough that
cruelty happens in its margins and nobody at the center notices or cares. The outpost is one of
those margins.

## Dwarves — The Older Foundation

The dwarves were here first, or at least their stonework was. They had holds and trade roads
before the human kingdom existed. As the hegemony expanded, the dwarves did not so much get
conquered as absorbed. Their holds became districts. Their trade roads became imperial highways.
Their engineers became the people who built the empire's infrastructure, fortified its borders,
and minted its coin.

Dwarves are integrated but culturally distinct. Respected but taken for granted, the way Rome
treated Greek engineers. They hold positions of trust in construction, mining, and finance
across the hegemony, and most humans would describe them as reliable allies without ever
noticing that "ally" is a strange word for a people who were building cities before your
kingdom had a name.

Tharr being the one still at the outpost when everyone else left tracks with this: dwarves
finish what they start, even when the people who ordered the work have forgotten about it.

## Orcs — The Misread Consequence

Orcs are rootborn. They grow from the earth at sites of great struggle: battlefields, collapsed
mines, places where something fought hard to survive. The land remembers what happened there,
and sometimes that memory takes shape. Orc tribes tend these growth-sites the way farmers tend
crops, returning seasonally to check for emergent orcs. No single orc has parents. The whole
tribe raises whoever the land produces. All orcs are nonbinary and do not reproduce sexually.

The hegemony's centuries of warfare have left battlefields, collapsed siegeworks, and mass graves
across the continent. These are growth-sites. The empire has been inadvertently seeding orc
emergence for generations and then treating the orcs that emerge as hostiles to be cleared. The
orcs see themselves as born from endurance. The empire sees them as a recurring pest problem on
former battlefields.

This is the core injustice of orc existence in this world: the humans created the conditions
for their emergence and then refuse to acknowledge them as anything other than a threat. Orc
culture teaches that they are shaped by the challenges they survive, and the most worthy survive
the most hardships. Those who attain both a long life and great triumphs command immense respect.
Other cultures see orcs emerging from places of violence and assume the orcs themselves are
creatures of violence. The orcs know they are born from endurance, not conflict. The land does
not honor the one who swung the sword. It honors whatever refused to break.

## Elves — The Other Side of the Table

The elves are not a single kingdom but a coalition of older, more established nations that
predate the hegemony by centuries. They have long memories and long lives, which means the wars
between the two powers are not history to them the way they are to humans. Elves who fought in
the last war are still alive. Elves who lost family in the first war are still alive.

The relationship between humans and elves is defined by a series of conflicts stretching back
generations. The hegemony expanded, the elves pushed back, treaties were signed and broken,
borders shifted. The current state is a cold peace, arrived at through mutual exhaustion rather
than resolution. Neither side won. Both sides lost people and territory they will not get back.
The wars ended not because someone surrendered but because continuing became more expensive than
stopping. The world feels emptier than it should because of the scale of those losses. Large
stretches of land between the two powers are just quiet now, and nobody is in a hurry to fill
them.

The cold peace holds because the alternative is worse, but forgiveness is not the same as peace,
and the elves have not forgiven. Trade happens at border towns and neutral ports. It is pragmatic,
not warm. Travel across borders is rare but not forbidden. An elf operating deep in human
frontier territory is unusual enough to be notable but not so alarming as to cause trouble,
especially this far from anywhere that matters.

The outpost sits in land the hegemony claimed during expansion but never meaningfully held after
the wars drew down. The elves consider it part of their buffer zone. The humans consider it part
of their frontier. Neither side has pressed the point because neither side wants to be the one
that restarts things over a crumbling stockade in the middle of nowhere. The party is building
something in a place that both powers have agreed, through silence, to ignore. If the outpost
becomes successful enough to be noticed, both sides might suddenly have opinions about who it
belongs to.

Elara being out here, an elf running a trading post on the human frontier during a cold peace,
suggests someone comfortable in the space between the two powers. Someone who has chosen the
margins over either center. Whether she left elven society by choice or necessity is her story
to tell.

Aldric's guilt is rooted in these wars. The things he did as a soldier were done to elves. Every
interaction between Aldric and Elara carries an unspoken history. She may not know his specific
deeds, but she knows what human soldiers did. He knows she knows. Neither of them brings it up.

## Gnomes — The Stranded

The Fey is real. It is a place where things operate on whimsy and literal meaning rather than
physics and logic, with its own kingdoms and powers and rules that mortals would call fairytale
logic. If you say "I could stay here forever," it might take you at your word. If you follow a
path that looks like it goes somewhere, it does, even if "somewhere" is a place that should not
exist. The Fey has its own peoples, its own conflicts, its own history. None of that is relevant
to this game. What is relevant is the door.

The Fey touches the mortal world in specific places for specific windows. The connection is
periodic but the pattern is long enough that mortal civilizations cannot predict or track it. A
clearing in a deep forest might shimmer with Fey light for a single afternoon every eighty years.
A cave mouth might open onto impossible meadows for a week every century. When the window closes,
the connection moves on. It may return to the same place eventually. It may not.

Gnomes are the ones who strayed through during a connection and were still on the mortal side
when the door closed. They wandered too far, stayed too long, explored one path too many, and
the Fey moved on without them. They are creatures built for a world where intention shapes
reality, stranded in a world where it does not.

**Why they are rare.** There is no gnomish homeland. No gnomish kingdom. Each connection deposits
a few at most, and the connections are centuries apart. The gnome population in the mortal world
is a slow, ancient trickle of stranded individuals, not a civilization.

**Why they seek novelty.** Gnomes come from a world that runs on change and meaning. The mortal
world runs on repetition and physics. New experiences, new places, new ideas are not hobbies for
a gnome. They are the closest thing the mortal world offers to the constant flux they were built
for. A gnome who has stopped seeking is a gnome in trouble.

**The Stillness.** A gnome who settles into routine, who stops looking for the new and the strange,
who lets the mortal world's rhythm replace the one they were made for, begins to slow down. They
get quieter. They stop asking questions. They stop caring about the answers. Eventually they sit
down somewhere and do not get up. It is not a disease. It is
not a curse. It is what happens when a creature of perpetual motion is forced to hold still for
too long. The Stillness is homesickness that kills, and every gnome in the mortal world carries
it as a possibility, the way a person carries the knowledge that they could simply stop trying.

**No shared culture.** Each gnome came through at a different time, from a different part of the
Fey, carrying different memories of a place that may have changed beyond recognition since they
left. Two gnomes meeting is notable but not necessarily a reunion. They share a language and an
origin and the common knowledge of what it feels like to be stuck, but they may have nothing
else in common.

**Why they are on the frontier.** The edges of the world, the untamed places where civilization
has not imposed its fixed rules, are where the connections are most likely to occur. A gnome
living in the hegemony's capital is someone who has given up looking. A gnome on the frontier
is someone who still believes the door might open again. The outpost sits in exactly the kind
of place where the Fey has touched the world before, and a gnome nearby would know that, even
if no one else does.

## Goblins — The Reclaimers

Goblins tell stories about the first of their kind springing from great refuse heaps, emerging
fully formed from the things that civilization threw away. Whether this is true is something
nobody knows for certain, including the goblins. It is their creation myth, and it shapes
everything about how they see themselves and the world. In practice, goblins are a biological
people who reproduce, raise children, and form communities. They mature fast, breed in large
numbers, and have short generational cycles.

Their population stays small despite this because goblin work is inherently dangerous. They are
scrappers and reclaimers and inventors, and the things they build from salvaged materials do not
always work the way they intended. Misfires, collapses, explosions, and chain reactions are a
regular part of goblin life. They are not particularly troubled by this. You build something,
sometimes it blows up, you build another one. The tribe carries on. The net effect is a people
who reproduce like rabbits and die from their own ingenuity at roughly the same rate, keeping
their numbers naturally in check without outside pressure.

**Culture.** Goblins are reclaimers. They see value in what everyone else has discarded. A broken
cart wheel and a rusted sword are not trash to a goblin. They are components. Goblin creativity
is not whimsy or decoration. It is survival. Everything gets a second life, recombined and
repurposed into something new that may or may not function as intended. A goblin tribe sets up
near an abandoned settlement the way a fishing village sets up near a coast: that is where the
resources are.

They organize into small tribes, rarely more than a hundred, centered around a strong leader.
Their communities prioritize play, creativity, and loyalty over productivity or order. They form
fierce bonds with those who earn their trust, and they have long memories for kindness in a world
that has shown them very little.

**Why they are rare.** Goblins are a small, tribal people with no kingdom, no infrastructure, and
no interest in being visible. The hegemony does not track them. Most people have encountered
goblin handiwork, a bridge patched with reclaimed materials, a tool that is clearly three other
tools welded together, without ever meeting a goblin.

**Why they are mistrusted.** Goblins are associated with ruin and decay. If goblins are nearby,
it means something failed nearby. People blame the symptom, not the cause. The hegemony views
them as pests in the same reflexive way it views orcs as threats: a nuisance produced by the
empire's own habits, misidentified as the problem rather than the response.

**Relationship to the outpost.** A crumbling, abandoned frontier outpost is exactly the kind of
place a goblin would find interesting. Whether the party encounters goblins as neighbors,
scavengers, or potential allies depends on how much refuse the outpost has been producing and
how long it sat empty before the party arrived.

## Leshy

Leshy are not a civilization. They are spirits called into plant vessels by powerful natural
entities. Each leshy is unique, shaped by its patron and the vessel it was given. Most people
have never seen one. Meeting a leshy at the frontier is the kind of thing you tell stories
about for the rest of your life, and half the people you tell will not believe you.
