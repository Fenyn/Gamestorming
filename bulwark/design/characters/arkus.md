## Vibe

Arkus is young and has never learned the rules that other cultures live by. They grew up in an
orc tribe where saying what you think is not rude, it is expected. If the food is bad, you say
the food is bad. If someone's plan is going to get people killed, you say that too, in exactly
those words, and no one takes offense because the alternative is dying.

This does not translate well. Arkus tells Aldric his armor smells like a wet horse. They ask
Fenwick why he puts so much effort into food that is going to be eaten in ten minutes anyway.
They inform Elara that her prices are unfair and then buy the thing regardless because they
needed it. None of this is meant as cruelty. Arkus genuinely does not understand that other
people wrap their thoughts in softer words before handing them over. They are learning, slowly,
that "honest" and "kind" are not always the same thing, and that most people prefer the second.

Underneath the bluntness is someone who cares more than they know how to show. They are young
and they failed at the one thing their culture asked of them, and they are trying to figure out
who they are without that answer. They throw themselves into work the way some people throw
themselves into prayer: completely and without reservation.

## Role in the town

Arkus runs the Smithy. They are the outpost's blacksmith, responsible for forging weapons,
armor, and tools. As the Smithy upgrades and Arkus gains access to better materials and
techniques, the quality and variety of equipment they can produce expands. They are also a
frontline combatant, bringing raw physical power that complements the party's existing tactical
strengths.

## Background

Orcs are rootborn. They grow from the earth at sites of great struggle: battlefields, collapsed
mines, places where something fought hard to survive. The land remembers what happened there,
and sometimes that memory takes shape. Orc tribes tend these growth-sites the way farmers tend
crops, returning seasonally to check for emergent orcs. No single orc is anyone's parent. The
whole tribe raises whoever the land produces.

This origin is the root of a persistent misunderstanding. Other cultures see orcs emerging from
places of violence and assume the orcs themselves are creatures of violence. The orcs see it
differently. They are born from endurance, not from conflict. The land does not honor the one
who swung the sword. It honors whatever refused to break.

Orc culture teaches that they are shaped by the challenges they survive, and the most worthy
survive the most hardships. Those who attain both a long life and great triumphs command immense
respect. When an orc comes of age, they must undergo a rite of passage to be considered a full
adult. The specifics of the rite vary by tribe, but the principle is the same: go out, face
something real, and come back changed.

Arkus did not come back. They were found wounded and unconscious in the forest by the outpost's
patrol, dragged back, and patched up. Whatever the rite demanded of them, they failed it. They
are too ashamed to return to their tribe, where they would be seen as a child still, unfinished
and unproven. So they stay. They pick up a hammer. They learn to shape metal the way the land
once shaped them, and they wait for the day when they have built enough, survived enough, to
feel whole.

## Arrival

Recruitable. Found wounded during a territory expedition. The trigger is mechanical as well as
narrative: the party clears its first forest expedition encounter, and Arkus is discovered wounded
and unconscious on the way back, dragged in by the same patrol that just proved it could survive out
there.

- Thematic parallel: Arkus's arc mirrors the modern experience of rejecting toxic patriarchal
  structures and finding an alternative path to identity. The rite is a system that defines
  worth through a narrow test of violence and endurance. Arkus failed that test, and instead
  of trying again, they build a life that proves the test was not the only measure. The
  outpost becomes the proof that there are other ways to be strong, other ways to be whole.
  This should be handled with subtlety, not allegory. The parallel informs the writing but
  should never break the fiction.
